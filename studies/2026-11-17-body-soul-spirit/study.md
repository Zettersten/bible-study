## Intro

This is a study about the parts of me that I usually don’t think about as separate things. I think about my body when I eat badly or sleep too little. I think about my soul when I get anxious or angry. I rarely think about my spirit at all, except as a vague word for “the religious part.” Paul doesn’t let me leave it vague. In 1 Thessalonians 5:23 he prays that the whole person, spirit, soul, and body, would be kept sound and blameless until the Lord comes.

The outline puts the question plainly: how do I walk in holiness so that I am ready when Jesus returns? Its answer is that holiness reaches all of me, not only what I do with my body but what I feed my soul and spirit. I’m a software engineer at HawkEye360, married to Wendy, and the father of Lucy, Lily, Michael, and Zoey. Most of my failures in holiness don’t show up as one big moment. They look like small, daily choices about what I look at, what I dwell on, and what I refuse to submit to God.

One note on the anthropology. The outline teaches a three-part view (body, soul, spirit) and describes the spirit as the God-conscious part of the person. The Reformed tradition has mostly treated man as body and soul, with the soul as the immaterial inner life that includes its relation to God. Paul’s language in 1 Thessalonians 5:23 and Hebrews 4:12 invites a careful distinction without turning it into three separate pieces. I’ll follow the outline’s terms, explain where I land, and name the disagreement honestly.

## Anchor Text

The two anchors are **1 Thessalonians 5:23-24** and **Galatians 5:16-17**. The first is the goal: the whole person, sanctified and kept blameless until the Lord comes. The second is the battle: the Spirit and the flesh oppose each other, and I need to walk by the Spirit. Both are bound to the same hope. Paul says God is faithful and will do it (5:24), and in the same letter he says we are to stay awake and be self-controlled (5:6). God’s faithfulness does not replace my watchfulness. It makes watchfulness possible.

## Q1. What is the difference between body, soul, and spirit?

**Answer.** Scripture distinguishes the physical from the immaterial. My body is the part of me that is seen, touched, fed, rested, and eventually buried. My soul is the inner life that includes my thoughts, emotions, reasoning, will, and intellect. That is what the outline calls self-consciousness: the “I” that knows it is someone. My spirit, in the outline’s framing, is the same inner life considered in its deepest orientation toward God, the part that can know him and commune with him.

Here I want to be careful, because the outline and the older Reformed tradition handle this differently. Paul’s prayer in 1 Thessalonians 5:23 names “spirit, soul, and body,” and Hebrews 4:12 speaks of the word of God penetrating “as far as the separation of soul and spirit.” Those verses encourage the view that spirit and soul are distinguishable in function. Many Reformed theologians have still treated man as body and soul, with the spirit and the soul as two aspects of one immaterial nature rather than two substances. The Westminster Confession speaks of man as consisting of body and soul, and the spirit language is usually read as the soul considered in relation to God. I think that reading takes both texts seriously. The distinction is real in the way the inner life works, but the Bible does not give me three rooms in a house.

So the practical difference matters more than the anatomical debate. Soul is the self that loves, chooses, and reasons. Spirit names that same self as made to know God and receive life from him. A person can have a rich soul, intelligent, emotional, and skilled, and still be spiritually dead toward God (1 Cor 2:14, which we’ll get to in Q6). When Jesus says to love God with all the heart, soul, and mind (Matt 22:37), he is not listing three unrelated pieces. He is describing the whole inner person directed toward God.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **1 Thessalonians 5:23-24** (CSB)
   > **23** Now may the God of peace himself sanctify you completely. And may your whole spirit, soul, and body be kept sound and blameless at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ.
   > **24** He who calls you is faithful; he will do it.

2. **Hebrews 4:12** (CSB)
   > **12** For the word of God is living and effective and sharper than any double-edged sword, penetrating as far as the separation of soul and spirit, joints and marrow. It is able to judge the thoughts and intentions of the heart.

3. **Matthew 22:37** (CSB)
   > **37** He said to him, “Love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your mind.”

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *pneuma* (πνεῦμα)** — “spirit, breath, wind.” The word is used for the human spirit, the Holy Spirit, and the wind. The common thread is life and breath that come from beyond the self. In 1 Thessalonians 5:23 the spirit is the part of me most directly open to God, and in 1 Corinthians 2:11 it is the inner knowing that no one else can reach. The payload is that my spirit isn’t a religious accessory. It is the part of me that was made to receive life from God.

- **Greek: *psychē* (ψυχή)** — “soul, life, self.” The same word means “life” in many contexts, including Jesus’ statements about losing one’s life. In Matthew 22:37 it stands with heart and mind as part of the total response of love. The payload is that the soul is the self in its full living reality. When the outline calls it self-consciousness, that is a fair translation of what the word covers.

- **Greek: *holoklēros* (ὁλόκληρος)** — “complete, whole, sound.” Paul asks that the whole spirit, soul, and body be kept *holoklēron*. The word means nothing is missing and nothing is broken. The payload is that God does not want a sanctified spirit with a neglected body or a holy soul with a corrupted spirit. He wants all of it.

**Personal Application.** This week I’ll write one line on an index card: “My spirit, soul, and body are one life, and God wants all of it.” I’ll read it before I open my laptop each morning at HawkEye360. At night I’ll tell Wendy one place where I divided myself this week, such as being present in the body at the dinner table while my mind was in a work thread. I’ll ask her to hold me to the whole person, not just the part that is convenient.

## Q2. What does “in our likeness” mean?

**Answer.** Genesis 1:26-27 gives two words. The first is “image” (*tselem*), and the second is “likeness” (*demut*). Together they say that humanity resembles God and represents him. The context shows what the resemblance is for. God says, “They will rule the fish of the sea, the birds of the sky, the livestock, the whole earth.” Being made in God’s image is not first a trait I possess. It is a calling to rule creation as a faithful representative of the Creator. An image in the ancient world was often a statue set up in a territory to show whose rule it was. Humanity is God’s representative on earth.

The Reformed tradition has long connected that image to moral and intellectual capacities: knowledge, righteousness, and holiness. The Westminster Confession says man was created in those qualities. Colossians 3:10 uses the same logic when it says believers are being renewed in knowledge according to the image of their Creator. Renewal is what happens when the image is restored in Christ. The image is not destroyed by sin, but it is distorted and needs renewal.

I want to be honest that “likeness” does not mean sameness. I am not God, and I am not a little god. The resemblance is real but dependent. When I say “in our likeness,” I mean that the human person reflects God’s character and rule in a finite creaturely way. That is why the question of body, soul, and spirit matters. The image is not only in my inner life. It is in the whole person who is supposed to exercise dominion under God.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **Genesis 1:26-27** (CSB)
   > **26** Then God said, “Let us make man in our image, according to our likeness. They will rule the fish of the sea, the birds of the sky, the livestock, the whole earth, and the creatures that crawl on the earth.”
   > **27** So God created man in his own image; he created him in the image of God; he created them male and female.

2. **Colossians 3:9-10** (CSB)
   > **9** Do not lie to one another, since you have put off the old self with its practices
   > **10** and have put on the new self. You are being renewed in knowledge according to the image of your Creator.

**Word Studies:**

- **Hebrew: *tselem* (צֶלֶם)** — “image, representation, statue.” In Genesis 1:26-27 the word is used for the image of God in which humanity is made. The word can refer to a physical image set up in a place to represent a king. The payload is that humanity is a living representation of God in the world, meant to make his rule visible through faithful stewardship.

- **Hebrew: *demut* (דְּמוּת)** — “likeness, resemblance, form.” The word appears with *tselem* in Genesis 1:26. It qualifies the image by pointing to resemblance rather than identity. The payload is the balance: real resemblance without equality. I bear God’s likeness as a creature, not as God.

**Personal Application.** This week, before I make any significant decision at work, I’ll ask whether this decision treats the people affected as people made in God’s image, or as numbers in a dashboard. When Wendy or one of the kids gets frustrated with me, I’ll remember that they bear the same image and answer them as one who represents God’s care, not as someone who wants to win the argument.

## Q3. What part does the body play in this triune nature?

**Answer.** The body is not an afterthought or a shell that I carry around until I can finally be spiritual. Genesis 1:26 says humanity rules creation. Ruling is done with hands, feet, voices, and time. God gave Adam and Eve bodies because the dominion he gave them was physical. The body is how the image of God gets done in the world: how I work, care for children, make things, and bless others. It is also how I sin. Paul says the sexually immoral person sins against his own body (1 Cor 6:18), which means the body is not a neutral tool for the soul’s purposes.

The body also belongs to God. Paul says in 1 Corinthians 6:19-20 that my body is a temple of the Holy Spirit, and that I am not my own because I was bought at a price. The word for body there is *sōma*, the whole person in bodily existence. The Spirit does not dwell in my spirit while my body is left outside. He dwells in the whole person. That is why Paul can say we should glorify God with our bodies.

The body has a future too. Philippians 3:21 says the Lord will transform the body of our humble condition into the likeness of his glorious body. The resurrection does not discard the body. It redeems it. So the body’s part in this triune nature is to be the place where holiness becomes visible now, and the place where God’s final work of redemption is displayed on the last day.

I think the outline is right to remind us that the body matters. Many Christians treat sanctification as an inner matter only, and the body becomes a zone of unexamined appetite. Paul would not accept that. The body is included in what God is making holy, and that inclusion is what makes it an honest question for the outline’s next points.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **1 Corinthians 6:19-20** (CSB)
   > **19** Don’t you know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit who is in you, whom you have from God? You are not your own,
   > **20** for you were bought at a price. So glorify God with your body.

2. **Philippians 3:20-21** (CSB)
   > **20** Our citizenship is in heaven, and we eagerly wait for a Savior from there, the Lord Jesus Christ.
   > **21** He will transform the body of our humble condition into the likeness of his glorious body, by the power that enables him to subject everything to himself.

3. **1 Thessalonians 5:23** (CSB)
   > **23** Now may the God of peace himself sanctify you completely. And may your whole spirit, soul, and body be kept sound and blameless at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *sōma* (σῶμα)** — “body, the whole person in bodily existence.” In 1 Corinthians 6:19-20 Paul uses *sōma* for the believer’s body as the temple of the Spirit. The word refers to the embodied self, not just the physical organism. The payload is that what I do with my body is an act of worship or a rebellion against the Lord who bought me. There is no neutral zone.

- **Greek: *holoklēros* (ὁλόκληρος)** — already defined in Q1. Its appearance in 1 Thessalonians 5:23 applies to the body as well as the spirit and soul. The payload is that all three must be kept sound, not just the parts I find spiritual.

**Personal Application.** This week I’ll move one habit that treats my body carelessly. Three nights, I’ll take a walk after dinner with Wendy instead of going straight to my laptop, and I’ll put my phone to charge outside the bedroom. Before I eat late or stay up late, I’ll remember that my body is a temple of the Spirit, and I’ll make one choice that honors that.

## Q4. Are all souls alike?

**Answer.** No, but they share one nature. Every human soul is an image-bearing soul, made by God and given a self that can know, love, reason, and choose. That shared nature is why the same gospel call applies to everyone and why every person needs the same Savior. But souls differ in temperament, history, wounds, gifts, and patterns of sin. Two people can hear the same sermon and respond in opposite ways because their souls are not the same. One is fearful and needs courage. Another is proud and needs humility. One is worn down by grief and needs comfort. Another is restless and needs direction.

Paul’s instruction in 1 Thessalonians 5:14 is a good map for this. He says to warn the idle, comfort the discouraged, help the weak, and be patient with everyone. The four categories are different, and the responses are different. A warning will crush the discouraged. Comfort will not fix the idle. Help may be the right thing for the weak but a trap for the proud. Patience applies to everyone, because everyone needs time to change. The point is not that I should label people in my head. The point is that I should not treat all souls the same way.

I also need to admit that I tend to think of my own soul as the normal one and everyone else as a problem to manage. That is a soul problem in itself. Paul does not ask me to sort people into categories so I can relate efficiently. He asks me to care for them, and he trusts the Spirit to work through patient and specific love. Hebrews 4:12 says the word of God can judge the thoughts and intentions of the heart. My job is not to diagnose anyone’s soul. It is to love them under God’s Word.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **1 Thessalonians 5:14** (CSB)
   > **14** And we exhort you, brothers and sisters: warn those who are idle, comfort the discouraged, help the weak, be patient with everyone.

2. **Hebrews 4:12** (CSB)
   > **12** For the word of God is living and effective and sharper than any double-edged sword, penetrating as far as the separation of soul and spirit, joints and marrow. It is able to judge the thoughts and intentions of the heart.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *oligopsychos* (ὀλιγόψυχος)** — “discouraged, fainthearted, small-souled.” In 1 Thessalonians 5:14 Paul names those who are discouraged with this word. Its literal sense is “little soul,” the soul that has shrunk under pressure. The payload is that discouragement is a real condition of the soul, and the church is called to comfort it rather than scold it.

- **Greek: *ataktos* (ἄτακτος)** — “disorderly, undisciplined, idle.” The CSB translates it “idle” and footnotes “disorderly, undisciplined.” The word describes someone who has stepped out of rank. The payload is that the response is warning, not contempt. Paul wants the disorderly corrected, because their disorder harms the body and themselves.

**Personal Application.** This week I’ll think about each of our four kids, Lucy, Lily, Michael, and Zoey, and write one sentence about how each one responds when corrected. I won’t make a theory of them. I’ll choose one specific way to be patient with the one who responds worst to correction, and I’ll talk to Wendy about whether she sees it the same way.

## Q5. What guidance do we have for dealing with different types of souls? Eph 4:2

**Answer.** Ephesians 4:2 gives four characteristics: humility, gentleness, patience, and bearing with one another in love. These are not techniques. They are the character of Christ worked into a community. Humility (*tapeinophrosynē*) is the refusal to treat myself as the measure of others. Gentleness (*prautēs*) is strength under control, the opposite of harshness. Patience (*makrothymia*) is long-suffering with people who are slow to change. Bearing with one another (*anechomai*) means putting up with what is hard in another person because I love them.

Paul then ties this to the unity of the Spirit (4:3). That matters for my approach to different souls. I am not dealing with individuals on their own. I am dealing with members of one body. The Spirit has bound us together, so my patience with a brother is not optional courtesy. It is part of keeping the unity the Spirit has made.

Read with 1 Thessalonians 5:14-15, the guidance becomes more concrete. The discouraged need comfort. The weak need help. Everyone needs patience. And the wrong I suffer should not be repaid with wrong. 5:15 says to pursue what is good for one another and for all. So the guidance for different souls is both gentle and firm. It is not permission to ignore sin. It is an invitation to meet people in the particular condition their souls are in and to keep doing good when it costs me.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **Ephesians 4:1-3** (CSB)
   > **1** Therefore I, the prisoner in the Lord, urge you to walk worthy of the calling you have received,
   > **2** with all humility and gentleness, with patience, bearing with one another in love,
   > **3** making every effort to keep the unity of the Spirit through the bond of peace.

2. **1 Thessalonians 5:14-15** (CSB)
   > **14** And we exhort you, brothers and sisters: warn those who are idle, comfort the discouraged, help the weak, be patient with everyone.
   > **15** See to it that no one repays evil for evil to anyone, but always pursue what is good for one another and for all.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *prautēs* (πραΰτης)** — “gentleness, meekness, humility.” In Ephesians 4:2 Paul uses the word alongside humility. The word describes strength that does not need to strike back. It is not weakness. The payload is that gentleness is what keeps me from using my correct opinion as a weapon.

- **Greek: *makrothymia* (μακροθυμία)** — “patience, long-suffering.” The word is about enduring slowness and provocation without giving up. The payload is that other people change on their timetable, not mine. The patience Paul asks for is a form of love that keeps showing up.

**Personal Application.** This week, when someone at work pushes back on my plan or a teammate makes me wait for a deliverable, I’ll respond with one gentle sentence before I say what I think. At home, when Zoey or Michael melts down at bedtime, I’ll take a breath, lower my voice, and bear with them for ten more minutes before I correct anything.

## Q6. Do unbelievers have a spirit? 1 Cor 2:14

**Answer.** Yes, and no. Yes, because every person has a spirit in the sense that 1 Corinthians 2:11 describes. No one knows a person’s thoughts except his spirit within him. The human spirit is part of what it means to be a person. Unbelievers have inner lives that think, know, feel, and choose. They are not animals that happen to have brains.

But Paul also says that the person without the Spirit does not receive what comes from God’s Spirit (2:14). The unbeliever does not have the Spirit of God dwelling in them. Paul calls the unbeliever a *psychikos* person, a natural or soulish person. The natural person can understand plenty of facts, but spiritual truths look like foolishness to him, because they must be evaluated spiritually. The problem is not that his spirit is absent. It is that his spirit is not alive to God in the way that the Spirit of God brings.

This answer matters for how I speak to unbelievers. I am not talking to a blank. I am talking to a person with a real inner life, who has the capacity to know and respond, and who needs the Spirit to open his understanding. That should make me both humble and hopeful. I can take my coworker’s questions seriously because they come from a real spirit and soul. I can also pray for the Spirit to do what I cannot do, because the Spirit is the one who gives understanding.

Paul adds in 2:16, quoting Isaiah, that the spiritual person can evaluate everything, and yet no one can evaluate him. I don’t read that as arrogance. It means that spiritual discernment comes from the mind of Christ (2:16), not from my own intelligence.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **1 Corinthians 2:10** (CSB)
   > **10** Now God has revealed these things to us by the Spirit, since the Spirit searches everything, even the depths of God.

2. **1 Corinthians 2:11** (CSB)
   > **11** For who knows a person’s thoughts except his spirit within him? In the same way, no one knows the thoughts of God except the Spirit of God.

3. **1 Corinthians 2:14-15** (CSB)
   > **14** But the person without the Spirit does not receive what comes from God’s Spirit, because it is foolishness to him; he is not able to understand it since it is evaluated spiritually.
   > **15** The spiritual person, however, can evaluate everything, and yet he himself cannot be evaluated by anyone.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *psychikos* (ψυχικός)** — “natural, soulish, unspiritual.” Paul uses it in 1 Corinthians 2:14 for the person without the Spirit. The word comes from *psychē* and describes a person living by natural human capacities without the Spirit. The payload is that the unbeliever is not missing a soul. He is missing the Spirit who makes spiritual things discernible.

- **Greek: *pneumatikos* (πνευματικός)** — “spiritual, belonging to the Spirit.” Paul sets this against *psychikos* in 2:13-15. The spiritual person is not one with a special inner glow. It is the person who has the Spirit and is taught by him. The payload is that spiritual discernment is a gift of the Spirit, not a higher IQ.

**Personal Application.** This week I’ll pray by name for one coworker at HawkEye360 who doesn’t know Christ. I’ll ask God to open a real conversation about what is true, not to win a debate. I’ll listen to what they actually think and ask one question about it, and I’ll pray that the Spirit gives them understanding in a way my arguments cannot.

## Q7. What incentive do we have to walk in holiness? 1 Thess 5:23

**Answer.** The incentive is the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ. Paul says the whole person must be kept sound and blameless “at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ” (5:23). The return of Jesus is the horizon that gives holiness its urgency. I will stand before him as a whole person, and I want to be ready when he comes. That is an incentive, but it is not a threat. It is hope.

The incentive is also God’s faithfulness. Paul prays that God himself will sanctify the Thessalonians completely (5:23), and then he says, “He who calls you is faithful; he will do it” (5:24). I do not become blameless by sheer effort. God calls, God sanctifies, and God keeps. Paul says in 5:9-10 that God did not appoint us to wrath, but to salvation through Jesus, who died for us. Holiness flows from that. The reason I want to be ready is that I already belong to him.

The Greek adjectives matter here. *Holoteleis* means complete, wholly, through and through. *Amemptos* means blameless, not open to charge. Paul is not asking for flawless performance. He is asking for a life that is complete in its direction toward God and unashamed before him. The outline is right that blameless does not mean perfect. Blameless means that the direction of my life is toward Christ, and that I am repenting, trusting, and growing rather than hiding.

I need this incentive because the other incentives are weak. Fear of getting caught, pride about looking good, and guilt about failing are all bad engines for holiness. The coming of the Lord and the faithfulness of God are a better foundation, because they put my effort inside a larger story that already has the outcome in God’s hands.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **1 Thessalonians 5:23-24** (CSB)
   > **23** Now may the God of peace himself sanctify you completely. And may your whole spirit, soul, and body be kept sound and blameless at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ.
   > **24** He who calls you is faithful; he will do it.

2. **1 Thessalonians 5:9-10** (CSB)
   > **9** For God did not appoint us to wrath, but to obtain salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ,
   > **10** who died for us, so that whether we are awake or asleep, we may live together with him.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *holoteleis* (ὁλοτελεῖς)** — “completely, wholly, through and through.” Paul prays that God will sanctify the Thessalonians *holoteleis*. The word combines the idea of wholeness with the idea of completion. The payload is that God’s sanctifying work is not partial. He aims at all of me.

- **Greek: *amemptos* (ἄμεμπτος)** — “blameless, without fault in the sense of not open to charge.” Paul asks that the whole person be kept *amemptos* at the coming of Christ. The word is used for an unblemished sacrifice in some Old Testament contexts, and it is also used for a life that is upright before God. The payload is that blamelessness is about integrity of direction and relationship, not an absence of every failure.

**Personal Application.** Tonight I’ll tell Wendy one reason I want to be ready for the Lord’s return, not out of fear but because I love him. This week I’ll stop one habit that I can point to as a sign of drift, such as scrolling late at night or snapping at the kids when I’m tired. When I do stop it, I’ll say to God, “You are faithful to finish what you started in me,” and I’ll thank him for one place I’ve seen that faithfulness this month.

## Q8. Can we sin in our soul and spirit?

**Answer.** Yes. The outline asks this, and the answer should be clear. Sin is not limited to the body. Paul’s list of the works of the flesh in Galatians 5:19-21 is full of sins that happen in the inner life: idolatry, sorcery, hatred, strife, jealousy, outbursts of anger, selfish ambition, dissension, factions, and envy. None of those requires a physical act. They are the soul and spirit turned away from God and toward self. Idolatry in particular is a spiritual sin. You can commit it in your heart while your body does nothing visible.

This is important for how I understand holiness. If I think sin is mainly physical, I may keep my body in check and feel fine while my soul rots with pride, resentment, or envy. I think that describes me more often than I want to admit. I can avoid every bad image on a screen and still spend an hour replaying a conversation where I looked good and someone else looked bad. That replaying is sin of the soul, and it matters to God.

The word “flesh” in Galatians 5 also shows me something. Paul includes idolatry and jealousy in the works of the flesh. So “flesh” is not simply the body. It is the whole person living for self apart from God. Sin in the soul is still flesh. The answer to it is not only bodily discipline. It is repentance, faith, and the Spirit’s work in my desires and thoughts.

Blameless does not mean sinless. It means that when I sin in my soul or spirit, I confess it, I receive Christ’s forgiveness, and I walk in the direction of holiness again. The gospel covers both body and soul.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **Galatians 5:19-21** (CSB)
   > **19** Now the works of the flesh are obvious: sexual immorality, moral impurity, promiscuity,
   > **20** idolatry, sorcery, hatreds, strife, jealousy, outbursts of anger, selfish ambitions, dissensions, factions,
   > **21** envy, drunkenness, carousing, and anything similar. I am warning you about these things—as I warned you before—that those who practice such things will not inherit the kingdom of God.

2. **Matthew 22:37** (CSB)
   > **37** He said to him, “Love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your mind.”

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *sarx* (σάρξ)** — “flesh.” In Galatians 5:16-17 Paul uses it for the desire opposed to the Spirit. In 5:20 idolatry and jealousy appear among the works of the flesh. The word is not only about the physical body. The payload is that flesh means the whole person oriented toward self apart from God. A pure body with a proud soul is still fleshly.

- **Greek: *zēlos* (ζῆλος)** — “jealousy, zeal, envy.” The word in Galatians 5:20 is about a jealous, competitive desire that grasps for what belongs to another. It can be used positively for zeal for God, but here it is among the works of the flesh. The payload is that a sinful heart can have strong passion for the wrong object. The problem is the direction of the passion, not its intensity.

**Personal Application.** This week I’ll confess one specific sin of the soul to Wendy: envy, resentment, or pride in my work. I’ll name it plainly, not as a general failure but as the exact thought that kept repeating. Then I’ll bring it to God by name each night, and I’ll ask Wendy to check me when she sees the same pattern showing up at home.

## Q9. How does feeding your soul affect the desires of your flesh? 1 Tim 4:8

**Answer.** Paul says in 1 Timothy 4:8 that “the training of the body has limited benefit, but godliness is beneficial in every way.” He is not saying the body is worthless. He is saying that bodily training, even good training, has a limit. Godliness reaches the whole person and carries promise for the present life and the life to come. So feeding the soul changes the desires of the flesh because it trains the self toward God, while the flesh trains the self toward itself.

Galatians 5:16-17 gives the mechanism. Walking by the Spirit means the flesh’s desire is not carried out. The desires of the flesh are not just bad impulses. They are a whole orientation that competes with the Spirit’s desires. Feeding my soul with Scripture, prayer, and truthful friendship gives the Spirit material to work with. Feeding my soul with anxiety, comparison, and entertainment that numbs me strengthens the flesh. The soul is always being fed by something. The question is whether I feed it with what is true.

Galatians 5:22-23 names the fruit that grows when the Spirit has his way: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. Those are not extra rules. They are the healthy desires of a soul fed by God. When my soul is fed by God’s word, the flesh loses some of its pull, not because I am stronger, but because I am being shaped by a better desire.

I also think Paul’s word “train” matters. Training takes repetition. I don’t become godly by one great decision. I become godly by repeated choices that shape my appetites. That is encouraging, because it means small faithful practices are not wasted.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **1 Timothy 4:7-8** (CSB)
   > **7** But have nothing to do with pointless and silly myths. Rather, train yourself in godliness.
   > **8** For the training of the body has limited benefit, but godliness is beneficial in every way, since it holds promise for the present life and also for the life to come.

2. **Galatians 5:16-17** (CSB)
   > **16** I say, then, walk by the Spirit and you will certainly not carry out the desire of the flesh.
   > **17** For the flesh desires what is against the Spirit, and the Spirit desires what is against the flesh; these are opposed to each other, so that you don’t do what you want.

3. **Galatians 5:22-23** (CSB)
   > **22** But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness,
   > **23** gentleness, and self-control. The law is not against such things.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *gymnasia* (γυμνασία)** — “physical training, bodily exercise.” In 1 Timothy 4:8 Paul uses the word for the training of the body. It is the root of our word “gymnasium.” The payload is that Paul respects physical discipline enough to name its limit. Bodily training has real but limited benefit compared with godliness.

- **Greek: *eusebeia* (εὐσέβεια)** — “godliness, reverence, devotion.” Paul uses it in 4:7 and 4:8. It names a whole way of life oriented to God in reverence and trust. The payload is that godliness is not a mood or a single act. It is a trained habit of the whole person toward God, and it changes what I want.

**Personal Application.** This week I’ll replace thirty minutes of late-night scrolling with reading one Psalm and one chapter of the Gospels before I go to bed. I’ll tell Wendy what I read the next morning, and I’ll ask her one question about it. The goal is not a spiritual performance. It is a steady feeding of the soul so that the flesh has less room to grow.

## Q10. Can you name some of the ways that you feed and train your spirit?

**Answer.** The outline asks a practical question, and I want to answer it without hiding behind generalities. Paul gives two verbs in this section. In 1 Timothy 4:7 he says “train yourself in godliness” (*gymnazō*). In Galatians 5:25 he says “keep in step with the Spirit” (*stoicheō*). Both are about regular, disciplined practice. The spirit is fed and trained through repeated, specific actions, not inspiration alone.

Here are the ways I can name. I can read Scripture slowly, not just skim a passage for a verse I like. Paul links godliness to the word of faith and good teaching (1 Tim 4:6), so I need to receive God’s Word, not just consume content about it. I can pray constantly and give thanks in everything (1 Thess 5:16-18), which means building the habit of turning to God at moments of stress, not only in a set devotional time. I can attend the church, sing, and hear the Word preached, because the Spirit works through the body, not only in private. I can confess sin quickly and specifically, so the spirit is not weighed down by hidden things. And I can take time to be quiet before God, because a spirit that never stops talking has little room to hear.

Each of these is a way of keeping in step with the Spirit. Paul does not say walk ahead of the Spirit or drag him behind me. He says keep in step. That is the rhythm of following. I know when I’m in step because my thoughts, my affections, and my actions start to line up with the fruit of the Spirit. When I’m out of step, I usually notice first in my reactions to people.

The honest list for me is short. Prayer is easy to say and hard to practice when life is busy. Scripture reading is easy to start and hard to keep. I need the discipline of a plan and the honesty of a friend who asks. I want to name those as practices, not as achievements.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **1 Timothy 4:7** (CSB)
   > **7** But have nothing to do with pointless and silly myths. Rather, train yourself in godliness.

2. **1 Thessalonians 5:16-18** (CSB)
   > **16** Rejoice always,
   > **17** pray constantly,
   > **18** give thanks in everything; for this is God’s will for you in Christ Jesus.

3. **Galatians 5:25** (CSB)
   > **25** If we live by the Spirit, let us also keep in step with the Spirit.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *gymnazō* (γυμνάζω)** — “to train, to exercise.” Paul uses the word in 1 Timothy 4:7 for training oneself in godliness. It is the verb from which we get “gymnasium.” The payload is that spiritual growth looks like training: regular, deliberate reps that shape the whole person over time. It is not automatic.

- **Greek: *stoicheō* (στοιχέω)** — “to walk in line, keep in step.” In Galatians 5:25 Paul says to keep in step with the Spirit. The word is used for walking in a file or in order. The payload is that the Spirit leads and I follow. The Christian life is walking together in the same direction with the Spirit, not a solo sprint.

**Personal Application.** This week I’ll memorize Galatians 5:25 and say it to myself before my first meeting each morning. I’ll read one Psalm before I check work messages. On Sunday I’ll go to church at Reston Bible Church with Wendy and the kids, sing, and listen to the sermon without checking my phone. I’ll write down one thing God said to me that week and share it with Wendy at dinner.

## Teaching Point: Ready for the Coming of the Lord

Paul reminds the church that Jesus is coming, and that the day will not surprise believers the way it surprises the world. The outline says the same thing: we may not know the day, but we can prepare by being sober-minded, vigilant, and walking in the light.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **1 Thessalonians 5:4-6** (CSB)
   > **4** But you, brothers and sisters, are not in the dark, for this day to surprise you like a thief.
   > **5** For you are all children of light and children of the day. We do not belong to the night or the darkness.
   > **6** So then, let us not sleep, like the rest, but let us stay awake and be self-controlled.

2. **1 Thessalonians 5:8** (CSB)
   > **8** But since we belong to the day, let us be self-controlled and put on the armor of faith and love, and a helmet of the hope of salvation.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *grēgoreō* (γρηγορέω)** — “to stay awake, be alert, watch.” In 1 Thessalonians 5:6 Paul says to stay awake rather than sleep like the rest. The word is about active watchfulness, not anxious staring. The payload is that readiness is a waking posture, a way of living attentive to what matters.

- **Greek: *nēphō* (νήφω)** — “to be sober, clear-headed, self-controlled.” Paul uses it in 5:6 and 5:8. The word describes a mind that is not dulled by intoxication or distraction. The payload is that the watchful soul is clear. Drunkenness, whether literal or spiritual, makes a person unready.

**Personal Application.** This week I’ll do a quick check each evening: what did I feed my soul today, and does it prepare me to be found awake when the Lord comes? If the answer is no, I’ll make one change before I sleep, and I’ll tell Wendy about it the next morning.

## Context & Theological Grounding

### Historical Context

Paul writes 1 Thessalonians to a church he founded under pressure. The letter is generally dated to the early 50s, and it is one of Paul’s earliest surviving letters. Its audience is a largely Gentile congregation that turned from idols. In chapter 5 Paul answers questions about the timing of the day of the Lord, and he is building on teaching the church already received. The language of spirit, soul, and body, the warnings about sobriety, and the call to watchfulness all belong to that pastoral setting.

### Literary Context and Passage Flow

- **1 Thessalonians 5:1-11:** The day of the Lord, watchfulness, and the call to be awake and sober.
- **1 Thessalonians 5:12-22:** Practical instructions for the church community, including encouragement, patience, and rejoicing.
- **1 Thessalonians 5:23-28:** The prayer for complete sanctification, spirit, soul, and body kept blameless.

The sanctification prayer in 5:23 comes at the end of the letter, after the practical instructions. It draws the whole letter together. The day of the Lord is the context for the holiness of the whole person.

### Trichotomy and Dichotomy

The outline teaches a three-part view. Some Christians call it trichotomy: the person is body, soul, and spirit. Others, including much of the Reformed tradition, hold dichotomy: the person is body and soul, with spirit understood as the soul in its God-ward relation. Both views read 1 Thessalonians 5:23 and Hebrews 4:12, and both affirm that the human person is more than the body. The dispute is mostly about whether the spirit is a separate part or a functional aspect of the immaterial soul. I lean toward the Reformed dichotomy while taking seriously the distinctions Paul and the writer of Hebrews draw. This is a question I want to raise with the class rather than settle by assertion.

### Image, Likeness, and Renewal

Genesis 1:26-27 gives the image of God to the whole human being. The Reformed tradition connects that image to knowledge, righteousness, and holiness, and it recognizes that sin has distorted the image. Colossians 3:10 speaks of renewal in knowledge according to the image of the Creator. Christ is the one in whom the image is restored. This is why sanctification is not a self-improvement project but the restoration of the image in Christ.

### Reading the Difficult Language

Hebrews 4:12 says the word of God divides “soul and spirit.” Some read this as proof of three distinct parts. Others read it as a poetic way of saying that God’s word penetrates to the depths of the inner person. I think the second reading is compatible with the distinctions I’ve drawn, but I don’t want to flatten the verse. The text is describing the power of God’s word, not providing an anatomy chart.

Galatians 5:17 says the flesh and the Spirit are opposed. That opposition is not between my body and my soul. It is between the self oriented to itself and the self oriented to God. Reading the flesh as merely the body would make Galatians 5:20 strange, since idolatry and jealousy are there.
