## Intro

Gary Hamrick opens with three things many Christians don’t fully understand: Israel and the Jews, spiritual gifts, and the Second Coming. I’d add a fourth, and it’s the one I keep coming back to: how can I know God’s will? Paul answers that question in this chapter more directly than almost anywhere else in his letters. He doesn’t leave it to mystical impressions. He tells the Thessalonians what God wants, names the specific behaviors, and then tells them what to do with their grief when believers die before Jesus returns.

Chapter 4 moves in three movements. First, Paul calls the church to holy living (4:1-8). Second, he calls them to love one another and work with their hands (4:9-12). Third, he gives them the hope of the Lord’s coming, so they don’t grieve like people with no hope (4:13-18). Holiness, love, and hope aren’t three separate topics. They’re one life, lived in light of Christ.

This study brings those questions into my house. What does God want from me, specifically? Who in our church needs me to love them this week? What does my work say about my faith? And when I think about Jesus returning, do I long for him or dread him?

## Anchor Text

The two anchors for this chapter are **4:3**, which answers “how do I know God’s will?”, and **4:16-18**, which answers “what happens when Jesus comes back?” The first is practical and personal. The second is cosmic and corporate. Paul puts them side by side on purpose. The same God who wants my body set apart also raises the dead and gathers his people.

The Luke passage is the Lord’s own teaching on the same return. Paul says, “the Lord himself will descend.” Jesus says, “It will be like that on the day the Son of Man is revealed.” They are describing the same day from two angles.

## Q1. Can you name two things that are God’s will for you?

**Answer.** I don’t have to guess what God wants from me. Paul says it in 4:3: “For this is God’s will, your sanctification: that you keep away from sexual immorality.” That’s the first thing, and it is not a soft suggestion. Sanctification is God’s stated goal for my life, and sexual purity is the first concrete line Paul draws. Then he names the discipline that goes with it. Each believer learns to control his own body in holiness and honor (4:4). He doesn’t exploit or defraud a brother or sister (4:6). God did not call us to impurity, but to holiness (4:7).

The second thing God wants is less exciting to me, and that’s probably a sign it matters. Paul says in 5:18, “give thanks in everything; for this is God’s will for you in Christ Jesus.” That verse isn’t in chapter 4, but it sits in the same letter, a few paragraphs later, and it tells me something about how to read the first. God’s will is revealed in his commands, and it is shaped by his grace. Thanksgiving is not decoration for a holy life. It is one of the marks of one.

Here is what I think is the most important point. Paul does not ask the Thessalonians to make themselves holy so God will accept them. He says God “has not called us” to impurity (4:7), and he says God gives the Holy Spirit (4:8). Sanctification starts with God’s call and God’s gift. That’s the indicative. The imperative, “keep away from sexual immorality,” rides on top of it. If I try to obey the imperative without the indicative, I end up with a self-improvement project that makes me either proud or hopeless. If I start with the indicative, I can obey with gratitude.

Verse 8 also has a sharp edge. Paul says that rejecting this teaching isn’t rejecting a man, it’s rejecting God. As an engineer I want to say, “Fine, but I’m not rejecting God. I’m just rejecting my pastor’s opinion.” Paul won’t let me off that easily. The instruction carries God’s own authority. So when I quietly decide some area of my life is exempt from God’s will, I have not found a loophole in the rules. I’ve decided God doesn’t get to speak there.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **1 Thessalonians 4:3-4** (CSB)
   > **3** For this is God’s will, your sanctification: that you keep away from sexual immorality,
   > **4** that each of you knows how to control his own body in holiness and honor,

2. **1 Thessalonians 5:16-18** (CSB)
   > **16** Rejoice always,
   > **17** pray constantly,
   > **18** give thanks in everything; for this is God’s will for you in Christ Jesus.

3. **1 Corinthians 6:18-20** (CSB)
   > **18** Flee sexual immorality! Every other sin a person commits is outside the body, but the person who is sexually immoral sins against his own body.
   > **19** Don’t you know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit who is in you, whom you have from God? You are not your own,
   > **20** for you were bought at a price. So glorify God with your body.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *hagiasmos* (ἁγιασμός)** — “sanctification, holiness, consecration.” Paul calls sanctification *God’s will* in 4:3. The noun names both the process and the state. Being set apart for God is not a private inner feeling; it shows up in what I do with my body, my money, my words, and my relationships. The cognate returns in 4:7 when Paul says God did not call us to impurity but to holiness. The same word runs through the whole paragraph.

- **Greek: *skeuos* (σκεῦος)** — “vessel, container.” In 4:4 Paul says each one should know how to control his own *vessel* in holiness and honor. Most interpreters take this as the body, and 1 Corinthians 6:18-20 supports that reading: the body is the temple of the Spirit and belongs to God. The CSB footnote notes an older alternative, “to acquire his own wife,” and some have read it that way. Either way, the payload is the same. Sexual self-control is a matter of holiness and honor, not appetite. My body is not a neutral tool that I may use however I want.

**Personal Application.** This week I’ll sit down with Erin and ask her to tell me honestly where she sees me least careful: late nights, screens, or how I handle temptation when I’m tired. I’ll ask her to pray for me by name. Then I’ll put my phone to charge outside our bedroom every night this week, because the late scroll is where my resolve is weakest. I’m not doing this to earn God’s acceptance. Christ bought me (1 Cor 6:20), and that is exactly why I want my body to show it.

## Q2. What are some of the ways that you can love other believers in the Leesburg area?

**Answer.** Paul begins with a compliment that is also a challenge. The Thessalonians don’t need him to teach them to love each other, because God has already taught them (4:9). The Greek word is *theodidaktos*, “taught by God.” It appears only here in the New Testament. Love among believers is not something we invent or manage like a program. It is something God teaches his children as they learn his character. Paul then says they are already doing it toward “all the brothers and sisters in the entire region of Macedonia” (4:10). Their love reached past their own street into the churches around them.

That changes the Leesburg question. The love Paul means is not limited to people I already like. It is covenant love that acts. John 13:34 gives the pattern: “Just as I have loved you, you are also to love one another.” Jesus does not say love the people who are easy to love. He says to love the way he loved, and his love went to the dying, the grieving, the doubting, and the betraying. Verse 35 adds that this love is the public evidence that we belong to him: “By this everyone will know that you are my disciples.”

So what does that look like in our area? I think it breaks down into things that are plain and practical. Pray for specific believers by name, not in general. Make room in the calendar, because love costs time. Notice the person who has gone quiet in small group. Pay attention to who is out of work, sick, grieving, or buried under a family crisis. Bring a meal, drive someone to a doctor’s appointment, sit with a couple whose marriage is hurting. Paul adds in 5:11 that we are to encourage one another and build each other up, which means our words count too. Encouragement is not flattery. It is truth that gives a brother or sister courage to keep going. Paul’s word for “do this even more” means love is not a finished project. It keeps growing.

I also want to be honest with myself. My default in the church is to love people from a distance. I will send an email, I will pray in the abstract, and I will think about helping. Paul is asking for something more costly: presence and provision. A brother who is carrying something heavy doesn’t need my good intentions. He needs me to show up with something concrete.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **1 Thessalonians 4:9-10** (CSB)
   > **9** About brotherly love: You don’t need me to write you because you yourselves are taught by God to love one another.
   > **10** In fact, you are doing this toward all the brothers and sisters in the entire region of Macedonia. But we encourage you, brothers and sisters, to do this even more,

2. **John 13:34-35** (CSB)
   > **34** “I give you a new command: Love one another. Just as I have loved you, you are also to love one another.
   > **35** By this everyone will know that you are my disciples, if you love one another.”

3. **1 Thessalonians 5:11** (CSB)
   > **11** Therefore encourage one another and build each other up as you are already doing.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *philadelphia* (φιλαδελφία)** — “brotherly love, affection among siblings.” The word joins *philos* (affection) and *adelphos* (brother). It describes the warmth of a household. Paul uses it for the church because the church is a family, and the family relationship is the basis for the love. The love is not a duty owed to strangers. It is the affection of people who share the same Father through Christ.

- **Greek: *theodidaktos* (θεοδίδακτος)** — “taught by God.” This is the rare word in 4:9. It places the source of love in God’s own teaching rather than in human instruction. Paul does not need to write them a manual because God has already taught them. The payload is humbling: when I struggle to love a believer, the problem is rarely a lack of information. It is a lack of attention to what God has already taught me in his Word.

**Personal Application.** This week I’ll invite one family from our church over for dinner with Erin and the kids. I’ll ask them one question I would normally skip: “What has been the heaviest part of your week?” Before Sunday I’ll also text one brother I know is carrying something hard and offer a specific help: a meal, an afternoon of yard work, or a ride to an appointment. I’ll name the help myself instead of waiting for him to ask.

## Q3. Tell me what this looks like in your life?

**Answer.** Paul’s words here are among the most practical in the New Testament, and I think they are among the most overlooked. He tells the Thessalonians to aspire to a quiet life, to mind their own affairs, and to work with their hands (4:11). Then he tells them why: so they may behave properly before outsiders and not depend on anyone (4:12). The sentence begins with ambition and ends with witness. That’s a surprising combination. The verb Paul uses for “aspire” (*philotimeomai*) normally describes the drive to be honored or to get ahead. Paul takes that drive and points it at something very ordinary: a calm, faithful, unhurried life.

I don’t think Paul is praising laziness or withdrawal. He is correcting something. In 2 Thessalonians 3:10-12, written soon after, Paul deals with people who stopped working and became busybodies: “They are not busy but busybodies.” Quiet work and minding your own business are not a retreat from the world. They are the opposite of restless meddling. A believer who works steadily, pays his debts, finishes what he starts, and keeps his mouth shut about other people’s business is a quiet witness. The unbeliever sees the difference.

Ephesians 4:28 supplies the motive. The thief must stop stealing and instead “do honest work with his own hands, so that he has something to share with anyone in need.” Work is not only a way to pay my bills. It is a way to have something to give. Paul does not tell the Thessalonians to work so they can earn God’s favor. They already have his favor in Christ. They work because grace produces people who are generous, dependable, and free from the pressure to be noticed.

So what does this look like in my life? As an engineer I’m tempted to aspire to the wrong kind of visibility. I want the good demo, the smart architecture decision, the praise from the team lead. Quiet faithfulness doesn’t get that. It looks like finishing the boring ticket, writing the status update that says what’s done and what’s blocked, and not joining the thread where everybody complains about the reorg. At home it looks like doing the repair before Erin has to ask, and not making a show of it. Paul’s standard is not that I become invisible. It is that the church should be known for integrity, not for its drama.

I want to be careful with the phrase “not dependent on anyone.” Paul does not say believers must never need help. He is talking about the posture of someone who is responsible for his own household. Sickness, disaster, and seasons of need are real, and the church is supposed to help. The concern is the habit of expecting others to carry what I could carry myself, or using dependence as a way to avoid responsibility.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **1 Thessalonians 4:11-12** (CSB)
   > **11** to seek to lead a quiet life, to mind your own business, and to work with your own hands, as we commanded you,
   > **12** so that you may behave properly in the presence of outsiders and not be dependent on anyone.

2. **2 Thessalonians 3:10-12** (CSB)
   > **10** In fact, when we were with you, this is what we commanded you: “If anyone isn’t willing to work, he should not eat.”
   > **11** For we hear that there are some among you who are idle. They are not busy but busybodies.
   > **12** Now we command and exhort such people by the Lord Jesus Christ to work quietly and provide for themselves.

3. **Ephesians 4:28** (CSB)
   > **28** Let the thief no longer steal. Instead, he is to do honest work with his own hands, so that he has something to share with anyone in need.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *philotimeomai* (φιλοτιμέομαι)** — “to aspire, to make it one’s ambition.” Paul uses the verb in 4:11 for the ambition of a believer. The verb normally describes competitive ambition. Paul redirects it. The Christian’s ambition is not visibility but faithfulness. The payload is a question I have to answer honestly: what am I ambitious for?

- **Greek: *hēsychazō* (ἡσυχάζω)** — “to be quiet, to keep still, to live without disturbance.” In 4:11 it describes the kind of life Paul wants. In 2 Thessalonians 3:12, the related noun *hēsychia* appears in the phrase “work quietly.” Quiet does not mean silent or passive. It means a settled, peaceful life that doesn’t stir up trouble. It is the opposite of the agitated busybody.

- **Greek: *euschēmonōs* (εὐσχημόνως)** — “properly, decently, honorably.” In 4:12 Paul says the quiet life makes the church behave *properly* before outsiders. The adverb is about visible respectability, but it is not respectability for its own sake. The Christian’s life should be attractive and credible to people who don’t yet believe. Paul wants the gospel to be commended by how we live, not only by how we talk.

**Personal Application.** This week I’ll finish the one work item I keep postponing, and I’ll send the status update without drama: what’s done, what’s blocked, and what I need. No complaint thread. At home, I’ll do the household repair I’ve been putting off, before Erin has to ask. I won’t announce it. The point of quiet work is that the people who need it can count on it, and the people who don’t see me doing it are still watching.

## Q4. What details do you understand about the rapture?

**Answer.** The word “rapture” doesn’t appear in 1 Thessalonians 4. It comes from the Latin Vulgate, where Paul’s verb *harpazō* in 4:17 is rendered *rapiemur*, “we will be caught up.” The English word stuck, but the Greek is more concrete. Paul says the living will be caught up together with the risen dead, in the clouds, to meet the Lord in the air. Before I try to add detail, I want to list what Paul actually says.

Paul gives me several clear details. The Lord himself will descend from heaven (4:16). He comes with a shout of command, with the voice of an archangel, and with the trumpet of God (4:16). The dead in Christ rise first (4:16). Those who are alive and remain are then caught up with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air (4:17). And the result is that we will always be with the Lord (4:17). Paul ends not with a timetable but with comfort: encourage one another with these words (4:18).

Christians disagree on how this event fits with other end-time passages, and I want to be honest about that. Many dispensational readers hold a pretribulation rapture: the church is caught up before a seven-year tribulation, and then Christ returns visibly with the church at the end of that period. Many Reformed and amillennial readers, including me, see 4:13-18 as describing the one return of Christ, when the dead are raised, the living are changed, and the whole church is gathered to meet him. The Westminster Confession treats the resurrection of the dead and the last judgment as one final event (ch. 32–33). The text itself supports the emphasis on a single coming. Paul says, “the Lord himself will descend,” not that the Lord will come secretly and then again later. Also, 1 Corinthians 15:51-52 places the change of believers “at the last trumpet,” which sounds like the final event.

The biggest thing I notice as I read this passage again is that Paul is not building a timeline. He is answering a pastoral concern. Some believers had died, and the Thessalonians were worried that the dead would miss out when Christ came (4:13-15). Paul says no: the dead in Christ will rise first, and the living will not precede them. That is the detail that matters most in this passage. Whatever order we draw on the whiteboard, the comfort is that no believer is left behind by grief.

I also want to be honest that I’m tempted to read this as a spec. As an engineer I want the sequence diagram. I can see how a timeline gets built from Revelation, Daniel, and other texts. But Paul does not give me the timeline here. He gives me the sound, the movement, and the meeting. If I make this paragraph carry more weight than it can, I end up with more confidence than the text provides.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **1 Thessalonians 4:16-17** (CSB)
   > **16** For the Lord himself will descend from heaven with a shout, with the archangel’s voice, and with the trumpet of God, and the dead in Christ will rise first.
   > **17** Then we who are still alive, who are left, will be caught up together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air, and so we will always be with the Lord.

2. **1 Corinthians 15:51-53** (CSB)
   > **51** Listen, I am telling you a mystery: We will not all fall asleep, but we will all be changed,
   > **52** in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet. For the trumpet will sound, and the dead will be raised incorruptible, and we will be changed.
   > **53** For this corruptible body must be clothed with incorruptibility, and this mortal body must be clothed with immortality.

3. **Acts 1:11** (CSB)
   > **11** They said, “Men of Galilee, why do you stand looking up into heaven? This same Jesus, who has been taken from you into heaven, will come in the same way that you have seen him going into heaven.”

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *harpazō* (ἁρπάζω)** — “to seize, snatch away, carry off forcefully.” This is the verb behind “caught up” in 4:17. Its root idea is sudden, forceful movement. The Vulgate’s *rapiemur* is the source of “rapture,” and the English word carries the drama of being snatched up. The payload is the sense of urgency and joy, not an escape hatch. The Lord does the catching. We do not climb up to him.

- **Greek: *keleusma* (κέλευσμα)** — “a command, a shout of command.” The noun appears in 4:16, and it appears nowhere else in the New Testament. In the ancient world it described a commander’s call to soldiers or the call of an officer to rowers. The CSB footnote renders it “a shout” with the alternative “command.” The payload is authority. The coming of the Lord is not a quiet slipping away from heaven. He comes with a call that carries command and triumph.

- **Greek: *apantēsis* (ἀπάντησις)** — “a meeting, a going out to meet.” Paul says we will meet the Lord in the air (4:17). The word appears in the New Testament only three times. Some interpreters point to ancient usage of the word for an official welcome, where a delegation went out to meet a dignitary and escorted him back into the city. That picture is attractive, and I find it encouraging, but I would not build a whole theology on it. The clear payload is that God’s people will go out to meet the Lord, and we will be with him in his presence forever.

**Personal Application.** This week I’ll read 1 Thessalonians 4:13-18 aloud with Erin, and then with the kids at dinner. I’ll ask them one question: “What does Paul want us to do when someone we love dies?” I’ll answer plainly. We grieve, but not like people who have no hope, because Jesus died and rose again. Before I go to bed I’ll write down the one fear about the future I most want to bring to God.

## Q5. Can you draw a word picture of what that day would look like?

**Answer.** I’ll try to draw it the way Paul describes it, without adding details he doesn’t give. It is an ordinary day. People are eating, working, driving to the store, putting kids to bed. Then the sky opens. There is a shout, an archangel’s voice, and the sound of God’s trumpet. The graves open first, and the believers who died in Christ rise. Then the living believers, who are still here, are changed in a moment and lifted up with them into the clouds. We meet the Lord in the air. And we stay with him. Paul’s final word is not chaos but reunion.

Paul gives me two details I want to take seriously. First, the change is instant. In 1 Corinthians 15:52 he says it happens “in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye.” Second, the change is bodily. Paul says this corruptible body must be clothed with incorruptibility, and this mortal body must be clothed with immortality (1 Cor 15:53). Philippians 3:21 says the Lord “will transform the body of our humble condition into the likeness of his glorious body.” So the picture is not a spirit escaping a body. It is a body made new and fit for the presence of Christ. I want to picture my knees, my back, my tired mind, and my anxious heart being made new, and I think that is part of why the picture is so hopeful.

I also want to picture Christ. Acts 1:11 says he will come “in the same way that you have seen him going into heaven.” The disciples watched him go up. The angels said he would come back the same way. The word picture must include a visible, personal, bodily return. This is not a metaphor for a nice feeling in the church. He is the same Jesus who was crucified and raised.

Here is where my engineer brain has to slow down. I can’t draw this precisely. I don’t know what the trumpet sounds like, what the clouds look like, or how the living and the risen are arranged in the air. Paul doesn’t tell me. What he tells me is enough: the sound, the speed, the change, and the meeting. If I fill in the blanks with my own imagination, I have to hold that imagination loosely. I may get the picture wrong in details but right in the substance: we will be with the Lord.

When I actually try to picture my own ordinary Thursday ending in that moment, I notice what I would be doing. Probably something unimportant. Maybe answering a message, or defending a point I should have let go, or avoiding a conversation I need to have. That is useful information. The word picture is not only for comfort. It is also a mirror.

   > **53** For this corruptible body must be clothed with incorruptibility, and this mortal body must be clothed with immortality.

2. **Philippians 3:20-21** (CSB)
   > **20** Our citizenship is in heaven, and we eagerly wait for a Savior from there, the Lord Jesus Christ.
   > **21** He will transform the body of our humble condition into the likeness of his glorious body, by the power that enables him to subject everything to himself.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *atomos* (ἄτομος)** — “uncut, indivisible.” Paul uses the phrase *en atomō*, “in an indivisible moment,” in 1 Corinthians 15:52. The word literally means something that cannot be cut into pieces. It is used for a moment of time that is too small to divide. The payload is speed beyond my ability to imagine. Our whole experience of change is slow. Paul says the final change is instantaneous.

- **Greek: *allassō* (ἀλλάσσω)** — “to change, to alter.” Paul says in 1 Corinthians 15:51 that we will all be changed. The verb speaks of a real alteration, not an end of existence. What is changed is the body. The payload connects to Philippians 3:21, where the body of our humble condition is transformed into Christ’s glorious body. The change is not the abandonment of my body. It is the redemption of it.

**Personal Application.** This week I’ll write one paragraph describing an ordinary workday of mine, and then add the moment of the trumpet at the end. I’ll write honestly what I would be doing in that moment and what I would regret. Then I’ll make one change that matches the picture: I’ll go to the person I’ve been avoiding and make the apology or the call before the day is over. Erin will know if I skip it.

## Q6. How is Jesus’ return like the days of Noah / Lot?

**Answer.** Jesus compares his coming to two Old Testament days, and both comparisons make the same point. People were going about ordinary life. They were eating, drinking, marrying, buying, selling, planting, and building (Luke 17:27-28). Nobody was expecting the end. Then judgment came suddenly. The flood took away the people who were not in the ark (Luke 17:27; Matt 24:39). The fire and sulfur fell on Sodom (Luke 17:29; Gen 19:24). The first similarity, then, is the normal life. The return of Christ will not be announced to people who are busy with something else. It will arrive into the middle of their Tuesday.

The second similarity is that the day separates people. In Luke 17:34-35, two people are in one bed, two women grind grain together, one is taken and one is left. Jesus does not say that everyone is in the same state. Something is decisive. The text leaves room for careful reading. The most natural reading in the context of judgment is that the taken are taken in judgment, the way the flood took away the unbelieving in Noah’s day (Matt 24:39), and the left are the ones preserved. Many Christians read the taken as the believers caught up to meet the Lord, as in 1 Thessalonians 4:17. I think the immediate context of Luke 17 favors judgment, because verse 37 talks about vultures gathering around a corpse. That detail should make me sober, not comfortable.

The third similarity is Lot’s wife. Jesus says, “Remember Lot’s wife!” (17:32). She looked back and became a pillar of salt (Gen 19:26). Jesus does not mention her to make us curious about her fate. He uses her as a warning about divided loyalty. She was leaving Sodom physically but her heart stayed attached to it. The return of Christ calls us to leave what is passing away and not cling to it. Verse 33 says the same thing in another form: whoever tries to secure his life will lose it, and whoever loses it will preserve it.

Where is the difference between Noah and Lot? Noah entered the ark before the flood, and the rescue was God’s provision. Lot was pulled out of Sodom before the fire fell, though the story shows him hesitant. Both stories show that God rescues the ones he has decided to rescue, and that his judgment is real. Jesus’ point is that the same mixture of ordinary life and sudden judgment will mark the day the Son of Man is revealed (Luke 17:30). The word “revealed” matters. His coming is an unveiling, not a secret event.

Here is the gospel thread I don’t want to lose. The people in Noah’s and Lot’s days were not destroyed because they were ordinary people eating and marrying. They were destroyed because God’s judgment is just. The good news is that Christ himself has taken the judgment that we deserved, so that the believer can wait without dread. That’s why Paul can say in 1 Thessalonians 5:9, God “did not appoint us to wrath, but to obtain salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ.” The Lord who will reveal himself is the same Lord who died for us.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *apokalyptō* (ἀποκαλύπτω)** — “to reveal, uncover, unveil.” In Luke 17:30 Jesus says that the day the Son of Man is *revealed* will look like the days of Noah and Lot. In verses 20-21 Jesus has just said the kingdom is not coming with something observable. The contrast is between a hidden or private coming and a public unveiling. The payload is that Christ’s return will be seen. It is not a secret rapture that only some notice. It is the unveiling of the King.

- **Greek: *apollymi* (ἀπόλλυμι)** — “to destroy, to lose, to perish.” The verb describes the flood and the fire: “destroyed them all” (Luke 17:27, 29). Then, in verse 33, the same verb appears in the statement that whoever tries to secure his life *will lose* it. The payload is a connection between judgment on the world and the self-protective soul. The people who wanted to secure their life by their own effort were lost in the same way that the people in Noah’s day were destroyed. I find this uncomfortable, and I should.

**Personal Application.** This week I’ll name the one thing I keep looking back toward that Christ is calling me to leave. I’ll tell Erin what I’m doing, because she is the one who will notice if I drift back to it. Christ does not call me to cling to what is passing away.

## Q7. When will this happen?

**Answer.** Jesus doesn’t give a date. He never does. The Pharisees asked him when the kingdom of God would come (Luke 17:20), and his answer was that it does not come with something observable, and that the kingdom is in your midst (17:21). Then, when the disciples ask where it will happen, Jesus says the vultures will gather where the corpse is (17:37). He answers the “where” question with a picture of judgment and certainty. He answers the “when” question with silence.

Acts 1:7 makes the same point. When the disciples ask whether Jesus will restore the kingdom to Israel at this time, he says, “It is not for you to know times or periods that the Father has set by his own authority.” The Greek words are *chronos* and *kairos*, which are often translated time and season. *Chronos* is the general span of time. *Kairos* is the right, opportune moment. Paul uses the same pair in 1 Thessalonians 5:1, “About the times and the seasons, you do not need anything to be written to you.” Paul says the Thessalonians do not need a schedule. They know the Lord will come. That is the knowledge that matters. Paul adds that the day of the Lord will come like a thief in the night (5:2). The timing belongs to the Father, and the coming will be sudden.

Matthew 24:36 says no one knows the day or hour, not even the angels or the Son, except the Father. I’m aware that the Son’s not-knowing is a hard text for some. The Reformed reading is that Christ, in his humanity, does not know the day, and that the Father has set it by his own authority. The passage is not designed to stir up date-setting. It is designed to call disciples to alertness. Matthew 24:42 says, “Therefore be alert, since you don’t know what day your Lord is coming.”

As an engineer, I want a timeline. I’ve been trained to ask for the deadline, the SLA, the release date. This passage will not give me any of it, and I have to accept that. It is not a failure of the text. It is the text’s purpose. If I knew the date, I might prepare for that date and then drift the rest of the year. Jesus wants me ready every day. The unknown timing is a kindness and a discipline.

What I can say is that the coming is certain, that it is visible, that it is sudden, and that the appropriate response is watchfulness. Watchfulness is not anxious staring at the sky. It is living as a faithful servant, doing what Christ has given me to do, and expecting him. Paul ends the discussion of times and seasons with the call to encourage one another and build each other up (5:11). Waiting for the Lord becomes the reason to love the church well.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *chronos* (χρόνος) and *kairos* (καιρός)** — “time, span of time” and “season, opportune moment.” Paul writes *chronōn kai kairōn* in 1 Thessalonians 5:1, and Acts 1:7 uses the same pair: *chronous ē kairous*. *Chronos* measures how long something lasts. *Kairos* names the appointed moment. The payload is that God has *kairos* in mind. The date belongs to his decision, and the church’s job is not to calculate it but to be ready for it.

- **Greek: *kleptēs* (κλέπτης)** — “thief.” Paul says the day of the Lord comes like a thief in the night (5:2). Jesus uses the same image in Matthew 24:43. The payload is surprise. The thief is not a warning about the sky. It is a warning about unpreparedness, the sort of carelessness that lets the house be broken into.

**Personal Application.** I’ll stop searching for dates this month, and I’ll do something faithful instead. This week I’ll write to the friend I’ve been avoiding, and I’ll schedule a one-on-one with each of the kids. Not “someday.” Christ may come at an hour I don’t expect (Matt 24:44). What I can do faithfully is the thing in front of me today.

## Reflection: How willing are you to live according to the will of God?

**Answer.** This is the question I want to ask myself before anything else. Paul begins by saying the Thessalonians already walk and please God, and he asks them to do it more (4:1). The Greek verbs are *peripateō* and *areskō*, and they describe a whole life. The honest answer is that I am willing in some areas and resistant in others. I am willing to let God shape my work ethic and my theology. I am less willing when his will touches my comfort, my screens, my desire to be right, or my need to control outcomes at home.

Here is the uncomfortable part. Willingness to do God’s will is not the same as being impressed by it. I can say “God’s will is good” and still keep one area closed. Paul does not ask whether they like his teaching. He tells them to walk and please God, which implies the will is concrete and the pleasure is relational. God is pleased when his children obey, and that obedience is an act of trust in his goodness.

The good news is that my willingness is not the foundation of God’s will. The foundation is Christ. In 1 Thessalonians 5:18 Paul says giving thanks in everything is God’s will in Christ Jesus. The phrase “in Christ Jesus” matters. God’s will is not a stranger’s demand. It is the Father’s purpose for those he has united to his Son. That gives me a reason to be willing. He has already given me the Holy Spirit (4:8), and he keeps working in me even when I resist.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **1 Thessalonians 4:1** (CSB)
   > **1** Additionally then, brothers and sisters, we ask and encourage you in the Lord Jesus, that as you have received instruction from us on how you should live and please God—as you are doing—do this even more.

2. **1 Thessalonians 5:18** (CSB)
   > **18** give thanks in everything; for this is God’s will for you in Christ Jesus.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *peripateō* (περιπατέω)** — “to walk, to conduct one’s life.” In 4:1 Paul asks the Thessalonians to walk and please God. The verb pictures a whole pattern of life, not one moment of obedience. It is the same metaphor Paul uses throughout his letters. The payload is that God’s will is a direction for my steps over time, and I am asked to keep walking in it, not only to decide once.

- **Greek: *areskō* (ἀρέσκω)** — “to please, to be pleasing to.” Paul asks them to please God. The verb describes an aim: a life that gives God satisfaction. This is not earning God’s love. It is responding to it. Because God has loved me in Christ, my life can aim at his pleasure, and a sinful life is a failure to aim at the right target.

**Personal Application.** This week I’ll ask Erin one question: “Where do you see me resisting God’s will in our home?” I’ll listen without defending myself, and I’ll write down one thing I will change. Then I’ll pray Paul’s words back to God: “that you walk and please God.” I want to be willing, and I need him to make me willing.

## Context & Theological Grounding

### Historical Context

Paul writes with Silvanus and Timothy, named in the greeting. The usual setting for the letter is Corinth during the second missionary journey, and the outline’s date of A.D. 51 is a reasonable approximation. The letter itself doesn’t give a year. The audience is a congregation Paul founded under pressure, made up largely of Gentiles who turned from idols. Some of their members had died, and they were worried about what that meant for the return of Christ (4:13). Paul had already taught them about the return and the day of the Lord (5:1). He is not introducing new material. He is pastorally applying what they had already heard.

### Literary Context and Passage Flow

- **1 Thessalonians 4:1-8:** Holiness, the call to sanctification, and the warning against impurity and wronging a brother.
- **1 Thessalonians 4:9-12:** Brotherly love, the call to do it more, and the quiet, working life that makes the church credible.
- **1 Thessalonians 4:13-18:** The comfort of the coming of the Lord, with the dead raised first and the living caught up together.
- **1 Thessalonians 5:1-11:** The day of the Lord, the call to watchfulness, and the instruction to encourage one another.

The chapter moves from exhortation to hope and back to exhortation. Paul doesn’t separate ethics from eschatology. The hope of the Lord’s coming is the reason holiness and love matter now.

### Canonical and Reformed Connection

The sanctification in 4:3 flows from God’s calling (4:7). The Reformed tradition has long insisted that justification and sanctification are distinct but never separated. In 1 Thessalonians 4 the same pattern appears. God calls and gives the Spirit. The believer obeys, and obedience is a real fruit of grace.

The return of Christ belongs to the wider canonical story. Acts 1:11 promises that Jesus will come in the same way he left. First Corinthians 15 explains that the dead will be raised incorruptible and the living changed. Philippians 3:21 describes the transformation of our lowly bodies into the likeness of Christ’s glorious body. The return is not an escape from creation. It is the final redemption of the whole person.

The Reformed confessions treat the resurrection and the last judgment as part of one great event. Believers wait for one return of Christ, one resurrection of the dead, and one final judgment. This is the framework I bring to 1 Thessalonians 4, and it shapes how I read the timing questions. I don’t need a detailed schedule to be confident about the central hope.

### Reading Difficult Texts Carefully

**The word “rapture.”** The term is not in the text, and it is a Latin translation of a Greek verb. I use it as shorthand, but I don’t want to pretend the English word carries doctrinal weight beyond the verb.

**Taken and left (Luke 17:34-35; Matt 24:40-41).** The sequence is ambiguous. The taken could be the rescued believers or the people swept away in judgment. The context of Luke 17, with its emphasis on the flood, Sodom, and the vultures in verse 37, favors judgment. Other faithful Christians read it as the catching up of the believers. I think we should hold the difference lightly and let the sobriety of the passage do its work.

**The timing of the return.** Christians disagree about the sequence of end-time events, including whether there is a pretribulation rapture, a posttribulation gathering, or an amillennial reading of a single return. I hold the one-return reading and believe the text supports it. But this passage is not a timeline. It is a comfort and a call to readiness. I can hold my view with conviction without making it the test of fellowship.
