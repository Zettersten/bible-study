# Personal Reflection Study - 2026-05-19

*A guided self-examination on Christian growth, marriage, family, and life goals.*

## Introduction

These six questions cut across the major arenas of a man's life: walk with Christ, covenant marriage, fatherhood as kids grow, vision for the future, and gratitude. They are not scored — they are diagnostic. They invite honest accounting of both faithfulness and failure, growth and drift. The goal is not to produce a performance report for God but to see clearly where Christ has been at work and where repentance and fresh dependence are needed.

## Q1. Specifically, what steps have you taken in the last 10 years that have led to your Christian growth?

**Answer.** Christian growth doesn't happen by accident. It requires intentional means of grace — not as merit badges but as channels God uses to conform us to Christ. Over a decade, the cumulative effect of small disciplines becomes visible: consistent time in scripture, community with other believers who speak truth, regular confession and repentance, the practice of prayer beyond crisis moments, faithful participation in the local church. Growth also comes through trials — seasons of suffering, loss, failure, or uncertainty that strip away self-reliance and force dependence on God. The steps that matter most are usually unglamorous: showing up when you don't feel it, opening the Bible when it feels dry, confessing sin when you'd rather rationalize it, staying in the hard conversation instead of walking away. Paul's language in Colossians 2:6–7 captures it: "walk in him, rooted and built up." The walk is active — you take steps. The rooting and building are passive — Christ does the work through means. Ten years is enough time to see the difference between performance Christianity (exhausting, brittle) and grace-sustained growth (slow, real).

**Biblical Support:**

1. **Colossians 2:6–7** (CSB)
   > So then, just as you have received Christ Jesus as Lord, continue to walk in him, being rooted and built up in him and established in the faith, just as you were taught, and overflowing with gratitude.

2. **2 Peter 3:18** (CSB)
   > But grow in the grace and knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. To him be the glory both now and to the day of eternity.

3. **Hebrews 10:24–25** (CSB)
   > And let us consider one another in order to provoke love and good works, not neglecting to gather together, as some are in the habit of doing, but encouraging each other, and all the more as you see the day approaching.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *peripateo* (περιπατέω)** — "to walk, to conduct one's life." The Hebrew equivalent *halak* (הָלַךְ) carries the same idea. The Christian life is not a series of mountaintop experiences but a long obedience in the same direction — cumulative steps over time.
- **Greek: *auxanō* (αὐξάνω)** — "to grow, to increase." Used of plants growing and of spiritual maturity. Growth is organic, not mechanical — it requires right conditions (soil, water, light) but is ultimately God's work (1 Cor 3:6).
- **Greek: *episynagōgē* (ἐπισυναγωγή)** — "gathering together, assembly." The root is the same as *synagogue*. Gathering with God's people is not optional for growth — it is the designed ecology for Christian formation.

**Personal Application.** Looking back over the last decade, the consistent disciplines that marked growth seasons were: (1) early-morning Bible reading before the house wakes up — imperfect but sustained, especially hard with Lucy, Lily, Michael, and Zoey's early years; (2) small-group community where I was known and could confess real struggles, not curate a version; (3) Sunday worship as non-negotiable rhythm, even when it felt dry or chaotic with four young kids; (4) marriage as a crucible — Wendy naming my sin and calling me back to the gospel when I drift into functional atheism ("God may be sovereign but I'll handle this myself"). This week, I'll revisit the last year and write down three specific ways Christ has grown me through hard things, not just smooth spiritual practices.

## Q2. In your marriage experiences, name the issues that have contributed both to your success and your lack of success as a husband?

**Answer.** Marriage exposes everything. Success comes from treating covenant as non-negotiable — not as a romantic feeling to sustain but as a blood-bought commitment that outlasts every season. When Wendy and I have done well over these thirteen years, it's been because: (1) we keep short accounts (confess and forgive quickly, don't let bitterness compound); (2) we assume good intent instead of mind-reading motives; (3) we make time to actually talk, not just coordinate logistics around four kids; (4) we pray together, even when it's awkward; (5) I lead by serving, not by demanding submission. Lack of success has come from the opposite: pride (refusing to admit I'm wrong), passivity (failing to initiate, spiritually or relationally), selfishness (protecting my comfort over her good), and neglect (treating her like a project manager instead of a co-heir of grace). The hardest truth is this: most of my failures as a husband stem not from malice but from functional autonomy — living as if my preferences, schedule, and goals are the default, and she is the add-on.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **Ephesians 5:25–27** (CSB)
   > Husbands, love your wives, just as Christ loved the church and gave himself for her to make her holy, cleansing her with the washing of water by the word. He did this to present the church to himself in splendor, without spot or wrinkle or anything like that, but holy and blameless.

2. **1 Peter 3:7** (CSB)
   > Husbands, in the same way, live with your wives in an understanding way, as with a weaker partner, showing them honor as coheirs of the grace of life, so that your prayers will not be hindered.

3. **Colossians 3:19** (CSB)
   > Husbands, love your wives and don't be bitter toward them.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *agapaō* (ἀγαπάω)** — "to love with covenant commitment." Not eros (romantic desire) or phileo (friendship affection) but the sacrificial,决定al love that chooses the other's good regardless of feeling. Christ's love for the church is the standard — and it cost him everything.
- **Greek: *synoikeo* (συνοικέω)** — "to dwell together, to live with." The prefix *syn-* means "together, with." Peter commands *knowledge-based* dwelling — *kata gnosin* (κατὰ γνῶσιν), "according to knowledge." A husband must *study* his wife, not assume he knows her.
- **Greek: *apotithēmi* (ἀποτίθημι)** — "to put away, to lay aside." Paul uses this in Colossians 3 for putting off the old self (anger, wrath, malice). The command not to be bitter is active — bitterness doesn't just fade; it must be consciously put away through confession and repentance.

**Personal Application.** One of my recurring failures is passivity masked as deference — asking Wendy "what do you want to do?" when I should be initiating. It feels humble, but it's abdication. This week, I'll plan one date night from start to finish (schedule, sitter, restaurant, conversation prompts) without asking her to co-manage it. And I'll confess to her the specific ways I've let autonomy creep in — treating family decisions as mine-by-default and only consulting her out of courtesy, not covenant.

## Q3. If you could change anything in your wife's behavior what would it be?

**Answer.** This question is a trap if answered wrong. The moment I fixate on what I wish Wendy would change, I have shifted from self-examination to blame-shifting — the oldest move in the book (Gen 3:12, "the woman you gave me"). The more biblical posture is Matthew 7:3–5: deal with the log in your own eye before obsessing over the speck in your spouse's. That said, honest marriage includes honest communication about hurt, unmet needs, and patterns that need to change. The key is the spirit: am I cataloging her failures to justify my own, or am I naming something real with love and humility? If I were to answer honestly: I want Wendy to relax. Let go. Be present in no-control. Drop the need to manage every variable. Accept imperfection — in herself, in me, in the house, in how the day unfolds. She carries the weight of four kids, a home, a thousand details, and the invisible load of making sure nothing falls through the cracks. That's real work, and I see it. But sometimes the grip is so tight it chokes the joy out of the moment. I want her to exhale. To sit in the chaos without fixing it. To laugh at the mess instead of cataloging it. But here's the rub: her vigilance keeps our family running. And my desire for her to "just relax" often translates to "stop caring about the things I don't want to think about." So the better question is: how do I honor and cherish the wife God gave me, and how do I shoulder more of the load so she *can* let go?

**Biblical Support:**

1. **Matthew 7:3–5** (CSB)
   > Why do you look at the splinter in your brother's eye but don't notice the beam of wood in your own eye? Or how can you say to your brother, 'Let me take the splinter out of your eye,' and look, there's a beam of wood in your own eye? Hypocrite! First take the beam of wood out of your eye, and then you will see clearly to take the splinter out of your brother's eye.

2. **Proverbs 19:14** (CSB)
   > A house and wealth are inherited from fathers, but a prudent wife is from the LORD.

3. **1 Peter 3:7** (CSB)
   > Husbands, in the same way, live with your wives in an understanding way, as with a weaker partner, showing them honor as coheirs of the grace of life, so that your prayers will not be hindered.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *dokos* (δοκός)** — "beam, log." Jesus uses hyperbole to make the point: your sin is massive compared to the minor faults you're obsessing over in your spouse. Self-righteousness blinds.
- **Hebrew: *sakal* (שָׂכַל)** — "prudent, wise, having insight." The word describes practical wisdom, not just intelligence. Proverbs says a prudent wife is *from the LORD* — a gift, not an achievement of your search skills. Gratitude, not complaint, is the right posture.
- **Greek: *timē* (τιμή)** — "honor, value, respect." The same word used for honoring God and governing authorities. A husband is commanded to *honor* his wife, not tolerate her. Honor means treating her as precious, not as a project to fix.

**Personal Application.** The log in my eye is this: I want Wendy to relax, but I haven't made space for her to relax. I want her to let go of control, but I haven't consistently proven I'll catch what she drops. I want her to be present in the chaos, but I'm often on my laptop while she's managing bedtime solo. This week, I'll do two things: (1) Identify one recurring responsibility she carries (meal planning, kids' schedule coordination, house logistics) and take full ownership of it for a month — not "helping," but owning it so she doesn't have to think about it. (2) Tell her directly: "I see how much you carry. I want you to be able to relax, but I know that starts with me shouldering more. What's one thing I can take off your plate?" And then actually do it, consistently, without needing to be reminded.

## Q4. Now that you have grown children, how do you see your relationship with them changing?

**Answer.** At 38 with four young kids — Lucy (8), Lily (7), Michael (3), and Zoey (2) — I'm not yet in the season of relating to grown children, but I'm watching the trajectory unfold in real time. Lucy and Lily are moving out of early childhood into the years where they start forming their own thoughts, friendships, and questions about faith. Michael and Zoey are still in the chaos-and-cuddles stage. The relationship is already changing: less direct control, more conversation. Lucy asks *why* now, not just *what*. The authority is still clear — I'm the father, not a friend — but the posture is shifting from commander to shepherd. In ten years, Lucy will be 18, Lily 17, Michael 13, Zoey 12. That's terrifying and clarifying. The seeds I plant now — family worship, the way I speak to Wendy, how I handle anger, whether I confess my sin out loud — those are the roots that will hold (or not) when they're teenagers and young adults. The temptation is either to parent by control (because it feels safer) or to coast (because it's exhausting). Neither works. Ephesians 6:4 warns against provoking children to anger — discipline without gospel, rules without relationship, harshness without warmth. The goal is not compliance but discipleship, and discipleship happens in the thousand small moments, not just the big talks.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **Proverbs 23:22–25** (CSB)
   > Listen to your father who gave you life, and don't despise your mother when she is old. Buy—and do not sell—truth, wisdom, instruction, and understanding. The father of a righteous son will rejoice greatly, and one who fathers a wise son will delight in him. Let your father and mother have joy, and let her who gave birth to you rejoice.

2. **Ephesians 6:4** (CSB)
   > Fathers, don't stir up anger in your children, but bring them up in the training and instruction of the Lord.

3. **Psalm 127:3–5** (CSB)
   > Sons are indeed a heritage from the LORD, offspring, a reward. Like arrows in the hand of a warrior are the sons born in one's youth. Happy is the man who has filled his quiver with them. They will never be put to shame when they speak with their enemies at the city gate.

**Word Studies:**

- **Hebrew: *shama* (שָׁמַע)** — "to hear, to listen, to obey." In Hebrew thought, hearing and obeying are inseparable. Proverbs assumes adult children still *listen* to their father — not out of coercion but out of respect built over years of faithful fatherhood.
- **Greek: *paideia* (παιδεία)** — "training, instruction, discipline." The word encompasses both teaching and correction. Paul's command to fathers is to raise children *in* the Lord's instruction — not just with rules, but with the gospel as the foundation.
- **Hebrew: *chets* (חֵץ)** — "arrow." The image is deliberate: arrows are crafted, aimed, and released. A father prepares his children for adult life, then lets them fly. But even after release, the trajectory you set matters.

**Personal Application.** Right now, with Lucy and Lily in elementary school and Michael and Zoey in the toddler years, the temptation is to default to functional parenting — keep them fed, clothed, on schedule — and miss the discipleship moments. This week, I'll schedule one-on-one time with Lucy and Lily separately (breakfast before school, or a Saturday morning errand) and ask each one a real question: "What's one thing you're learning about God right now?" or "What's something hard you're facing that we can pray about together?" And with Michael and Zoey, I'll be more intentional about narrating the gospel in the small moments — bedtime prayers, discipline, when they see me fail and confess.

## Q5. What are your personal goals for the next ten years?

**Answer.** Goals without the fear of the Lord become idols. Proverbs 19:21 cuts through every five-year plan: "Many plans are in a person's heart, but the LORD's decree will prevail." That doesn't mean planning is wrong — it means planning must be held loosely, submitted to God's sovereignty, and grounded in kingdom priorities, not cultural scorecards. At 38, the next ten years are pivotal — Lucy will be 18, Lily 17, Michael 13, Zoey 12. Here's what I'm aiming for: **Near-term:** (1) Read the Bible cover to cover in CSB — not devotional snippets but sustained, sequential reading. (2) One-on-one time with each kid — individual dates and hangouts, not just family logistics. (3) Family trips and vacations — making memories while they're still young enough to want to come. **Longer-term:** (4) Encourage and support the kids' growth in scripture — guide their understanding, help them love the Bible. (5) Lead the family through difficulty with a Christ-like mentality — when trials come (and they will), anchor in the gospel, not panic or passivity. **Life priorities:** (6) Work less, play more — shift away from work-as-identity. (7) 10x the sex — honest goal. Marriage intimacy takes a hit with four young kids; I want to fight for it, not let it drift. (8) More guys nights — invite guys over for fellowship and firepits at the house, not just attend scheduled events. (9) Missions — Brazil again, maybe taking one or two of the kids this time. (10) Make more money — yes, this sits in tension with "work less." I'm aware. The goal is work smarter, not just grind harder. James 4:13–15 warns against arrogant planning: "You don't even know what tomorrow will bring." The posture is humble intentionality: make plans, hold them with open hands, ready to pivot when God redirects.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **Proverbs 19:21** (CSB)
   > Many plans are in a person's heart, but the LORD's decree will prevail.

2. **James 4:13–15** (CSB)
   > Come now, you who say, "Today or tomorrow we will travel to such and such a city and spend a year there and do business and make a profit." Yet you do not know what tomorrow will bring—what your life will be! For you are like a vapor that appears for a little while, then vanishes. Instead, you should say, "If the Lord wills, we will live and do this or that."

3. **Matthew 6:33** (CSB)
   > But seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be provided for you.

**Word Studies:**

- **Hebrew: *machashavah* (מַחֲשָׁבָה)** — "thought, plan, purpose." The word captures both intent and scheme. Humans plan constantly — the question is whether those plans are submitted to God's *etsah* (עֵצָה), his counsel or decree, which cannot be overturned.
- **Greek: *atmis* (ἀτμίς)** — "vapor, mist, breath." James uses the image to humble us: life is short, fragile, transient. Ten-year plans are meaningful, but they are still mist. Memento mori — remember you will die.
- **Greek: *basileia* (βασιλεία)** — "kingdom, reign, rule." Jesus' priority reorders everything: seek *God's* kingdom, not your own empire. When kingdom priorities come first, the other things (provision, purpose, direction) are added — not as primary goals but as byproducts of right ordering.

**Personal Application.** These ten goals sit in front of me now, written down. Some of them live in tension: "work less" and "make more money" don't resolve cleanly. "10x the sex" and "more guys nights" both require intentionality and time I don't have infinite supply of. The Brazil missions trip with kids sounds heroic until I remember Michael will be 13 and Zoey 12 — is that the right age, or do I wait? The point is this: I have plans, but they are not promises. This week, I'll do two things: (1) Tape this list to the inside cover of my CSB Bible as a bookmark — not as a contract with God but as a prayer I'm bringing before him daily. (2) Share these goals with Wendy and ask her: "What am I missing? What's naive? What needs to change?" Because if my goals don't have her input and support, they're not family goals — they're my goals imposed on the family. And I'll pray Proverbs 19:21 over all of it: "Lord, these are my plans, but your decree will prevail. Make me ready to pivot."

## Q6. What are the 5 things that you are most thankful for?

**Answer.** Gratitude is not optional for Christians — it is the diagnostic vital sign of a gospel-shaped heart. Paul links gratitude to every major doctrine: election (Col 1:12), perseverance (Col 2:7), sanctification (Col 3:15–17), prayer (Col 4:2). When gratitude fades, it signals drift toward entitlement, autonomy, or bitterness. The five things I'm most thankful for aren't abstract categories — they're concrete, visible mercies God has given: (1) **My family** — Wendy, Lucy, Lily, Michael, Zoey. Healthy. Beautiful. Fun. They love the Lord. I feel blessed, and that's not hyperbole. Every morning I wake up to noise and chaos and life, and it's a gift I don't deserve. (2) **My career** — Engineering work that pays well enough to keep Wendy home with the kids. Not just survival income but provision that supports four growing kids and puts us close enough to the city for opportunity but not so close we're crushed by the cost. (3) **My home** — A big house with a wood shop where I can build things, a large pool where the kids play all summer, a treehouse they climb, and a neighborhood where we can walk to the local brewery, parks, ice cream parlors. It's more than shelter — it's a place where family life happens. (4) **My friends and community** — The older guys in this bible study (you know who you are), dads from church, guys I go to the gym with. Friendships that both deposit and withdraw — mutual, not transactional. Iron sharpening iron. (5) **Wendy, my wife** — Yes, she's already in #1, but she gets her own line. Covenant partner, co-heir of grace, the one who sees me at my worst and still chooses to stay. These five aren't the most spiritual-sounding answers, but they're honest. And honesty before God is the starting point for real gratitude.

**Biblical Support:**

1. **1 Thessalonians 5:16–18** (CSB)
   > Rejoice always, pray constantly, give thanks in everything; for this is God's will for you in Christ Jesus.

2. **Colossians 3:15–17** (CSB)
   > And let the peace of Christ, to which you were also called in one body, rule your hearts. And be thankful. Let the word of Christ dwell richly among you, in all wisdom teaching and admonishing one another through psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs, singing to God with gratitude in your hearts. And whatever you do, in word or in deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him.

3. **Psalm 103:2–5** (CSB)
   > My soul, bless the LORD, and do not forget all his benefits. He forgives all your iniquity; he heals all your diseases. He redeems your life from the Pit; he crowns you with faithful love and compassion. He satisfies you with good things; your youth is renewed like the eagle.

**Word Studies:**

- **Greek: *eucharisteo* (εὐχαριστέω)** — "to give thanks." The root is *charis* (χάρις), grace. Thanksgiving is the response to grace received. The word is used over 30 times in Paul's letters — gratitude is not decorative, it's structural to Christian life.
- **Greek: *eucharisteō* (εὐχαριστῶ) / *eucharistia* (εὐχαριστία)** — "thanksgiving, gratitude." The word behind *Eucharist* — the Lord's Supper, where we give thanks for Christ's body and blood. Every act of thanksgiving echoes the table.
- **Hebrew: *barak* (בָּרַךְ)** — "to bless, to kneel, to praise." The Psalmist commands his own soul to bless the LORD — gratitude is not a feeling to wait for but a posture to command yourself into. And it begins with *not forgetting* God's benefits.

**Personal Application.** I drift toward ingratitude faster than I admit — eyes on what I don't have, what's broken, what could be better. The engineering mindset is always optimizing, fixing, improving — which is good for code but toxic for gratitude. This week, I'll write out the five things above (family, career, home, community, Wendy) on a notecard and pray through them out loud every morning before opening my laptop. Not rushed, not performative — actually pause and name what God has given. And I'll do two things: (1) Tell Wendy directly, face-to-face, not in passing: "I'm grateful God gave me you" — and name one specific way she's been a gift this week. (2) At the next bible study or church gathering, I'll share one of these five out loud as public gratitude — not as a testimony performance but as witness that God is generous. Gratitude grows when spoken, not just felt.
