# In This World But Not of This World

**Passage:** Daniel 1  
**Date:** June 30, 2026  
**Translation:** CSB

---

## Introduction

Daniel's story hits differently when you realize what was actually happening. This wasn't capture and enslavement — that might've been simpler. This was systematic, state-sponsored identity erasure. Name change. Wardrobe change. Language immersion. Religious indoctrination. A three-year program designed to produce Babylonian bureaucrats out of Hebrew teenagers.

And it worked on most of them. But not Daniel.

The question isn't whether we face the same pressure. We do — every screen, every HR training, every "just go along to get along" compromise. The question is whether we'll decide *before* the moment what we're willing to lose.

This chapter shows us how a teenager in exile became the man who'd later choose a lion's den over compromise. It didn't start in chapter 6. It started here, in the small no that nobody but God saw coming.

## Q1. Can you give an example from your own life of deciding before temptations?

### Answer

Daniel "determined" (v. 8) before the food ever showed up. That's the key. By the time the tray hit the table, the decision was already made. Most of us fail not because we're weak in the moment but because we've never decided beforehand what line we won't cross.

I decided before Lucy and Lily started school that I wouldn't complain about teachers in front of them. Not because every teacher's been great — some haven't. But because I don't want them learning that authority is only worth respecting when it's convenient. That decision's been tested. Multiple times. But it was made before the test showed up, which is the only reason it held.

The same goes for money. Wendy and I decided years ago — broke, newlyweds — that we'd tithe first, save second, spend what's left. That call wasn't made month-by-month based on how we felt. It was made once, and the monthly execution is just obedience to an old decision. The alternative is re-litigating it every time the HVAC breaks or the minivan needs tires.

Daniel's not heroic in verse 8 because of what he did in that moment. He's heroic because of what he'd already decided about who God is and what faithfulness costs. By the time Babylon rolled out the royal menu, the war was already won.

### Biblical Support

**Proverbs 22:3** (CSB)
> A sensible person sees danger and takes cover, but the inexperienced keep going and are punished.

**Word Study:** *ʿārûm* (עָרוּם) — "prudent, shrewd, sensible." Not mere caution but foresight that prepares for what's coming. Wisdom anticipates temptation and builds the fence before the cliff appears.

**1 Corinthians 10:13** (CSB)
> No temptation has come upon you except what is common to humanity. But God is faithful; he will not allow you to be tempted beyond what you are able, but with the temptation he will also provide a way out so that you may be able to bear it.

**Word Study:** *ekbasis* (ἔκβασις) — "a way out, an escape route." The promise isn't that temptation won't come; it's that God engineers the exit before the trap closes. Daniel's predecision was the exit God provided.

**Psalm 119:11** (CSB)
> I have treasured your word in my heart so that I may not sin against you.

**Word Study:** *tsāphan* (צָפַן) — "to hide, to treasure, to store up." The idea is preemptive storage — laying up truth before the moment of need, like ammunition stockpiled before the battle.

### Personal Application

This week, decide now what you'll do the next time Wendy asks if you have margin to help with bedtime and you're mid-project. Don't wait for the ask. Decide now: project pauses, kids come first. Write it on a sticky note and put it on your monitor if you have to, but make the call before the moment forces it.

## Q2. What can we learn from his approach?

### Answer

Daniel didn't stage a protest. He didn't refuse to show up for the indoctrination program. He didn't lecture Ashpenaz about Levitical dietary law. He asked. Respectfully. And then proposed a test that let the official save face and his own neck.

This is genius-level cultural engagement. Daniel knew he was the foreigner in a hostile system, and he acted like it. He didn't demand his rights. He had none. He didn't invoke religious freedom. That wasn't a thing. He leveraged what he did have — a relationship with the chief eunuch (v. 9, "God had granted Daniel kindness"), a reasonable proposal, and trust that God could make vegetables outperform filet mignon.

We're often so scared of compromise that we mistake belligerence for faithfulness. Daniel shows us a third way: be clear about the line, be respectful about the ask, and let God handle the outcome. The line was "I won't defile myself." The ask was "test me." The outcome was God's department.

There's a time to flip tables and call the Pharisees whitewashed tombs. But most of the time, especially when you're the minority in someone else's house, the godly move is Daniel's: speak truth, ask humbly, and trust the results to the One who granted you favor in the first place.

### Biblical Support

**Romans 12:18** (CSB)
> If possible, as far as it depends on you, live at peace with everyone.

**Word Study:** *eirēneuō* (εἰρηνεύω) — "to be at peace, to keep peace." The "if possible" qualifier matters — Paul's not commanding pacifism at any cost. But where faithfulness allows it, pursue peace. Daniel drew a line and still kept the peace.

**1 Peter 3:15** (CSB)
> but in your hearts regard Christ the Lord as holy, ready at any time to give a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you. Yet do this with gentleness and respect,

**Word Study:** *prautēs* (πραΰτης) — "gentleness, meekness, humility." Not weakness — strength under control. Daniel had conviction. He also had manners.

**Colossians 4:5-6** (CSB)
> Act wisely toward outsiders, making the most of the time. Let your speech always be gracious, seasoned with salt, so that you may know how you should answer each person.

**Word Study:** *charis* (χάρις) — "grace, favor, kindness." Speech "seasoned with salt" preserves truth while making it palatable. Daniel's ask was salty — it preserved the boundary — but gracious enough that the guard said yes.

### Personal Application

Next time someone at work pushes you toward something that violates conscience — whether it's fudging a timeline, signing off on sloppy code, or sitting through a DEI session that treats the gospel like bigotry — don't blow up. Ask: "Can we try this approach instead?" Propose the alternative. Let your no be embedded in a respectful yes to something else. If they say no to that, you've got your answer. But don't skip the ask.

## Q3. What is the lesson here?

### Answer

God vindicated Daniel. Not symbolically or spiritually — physically. Ten days in, Daniel and his friends looked better and healthier than the guys eating from the king's table. That's not a metaphor. That's measurable, visible, undeniable proof that faithfulness doesn't cost you what the world says it will.

Here's the lesson: God doesn't owe you this outcome. He could've let Daniel look gaunt. He could've let the guard panic and force-feed him pork. He didn't, but He could have. The lesson isn't "obey and you'll always win the metrics." The lesson is "obey, and leave the metrics to God."

Sometimes faithfulness makes you look like the smartest guy in the room (Daniel chapter 1). Sometimes it gets you thrown in a furnace (Daniel chapter 3) or a lion's den (Daniel chapter 6). God's vindication doesn't always show up in ten days. Sometimes it shows up in seventy years when your great-grandkids read your story and thank God you didn't bend.

But here? Here God put His finger on the scale. He made it undeniable. And that's grace — not just for Daniel, but for Ashpenaz and the guard, who now had evidence that Daniel's God was real and Daniel's obedience wasn't insane.

### Biblical Support

**Matthew 6:33** (CSB)
> But seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be provided for you.

**Word Study:** *prostithēmi* (προστίθημι) — "to add, to give in addition." The promise isn't that you get what you want if you pray hard enough. It's that when you pursue God first, He adds what you actually need. Daniel sought God's holiness first. God added health.

**Proverbs 3:5-6** (CSB)
> Trust in the LORD with all your heart, and do not rely on your own understanding; in all your ways know him, and he will make your paths straight.

**Word Study:** *bāṭaḥ* (בָּטַח) — "to trust, to be confident, to feel secure." Not wishful thinking but settled confidence that God's got it. Daniel didn't know how the test would go. He trusted God with the how.

**Hebrews 11:6** (CSB)
> Now without faith it is impossible to please God, since the one who draws near to him must believe that he exists and that he rewards those who seek him.

**Word Study:** *misthapodotēs* (μισθαποδότης) — "rewarder, one who pays wages." God isn't arbitrary. He sees. He responds. Sometimes immediately (Daniel 1), sometimes after death (Hebrews 11:13). But He rewards.

### Personal Application

This week, pick one expense you've been justifying because "everyone does it" — the subscription you don't use, the convenience purchase that's become a habit, the "I deserve this" buy. Cut it. Put that money toward something kingdom-facing — maybe the next missions trip fund for the kids, maybe a need at church you've been ignoring. Don't announce it. Just do it, and watch whether God provides what you actually need in ways you didn't engineer.

## Q4. The lesson for us?

### Answer

Daniel learned Chaldean. He studied Babylonian literature. He mastered their administrative system. He didn't boycott the education or refuse to engage the culture. He just refused to let it own him.

This wrecks the "Benedict Option" retreat-from-culture posture and the "total transformation" drown-in-culture posture at the same time. Daniel didn't build a compound and homeschool in Hebrew. He also didn't let Babylon rename his God. He worked the system, learned the language, and kept his soul.

We need this. Our kids go to public school. They'll learn things we don't agree with. That's not the crisis. The crisis is if they learn those things and we've never taught them how to filter poison from food. The lesson from Daniel is: let them learn the language of the empire, but make sure they know the language of Zion better.

Same goes for work. You can be the best software engineer in your company and refuse to ship a feature that surveils users. You can be the top salesman and refuse to lie about the product. You can learn Chaldean and stay Hebrew.

God gave Daniel understanding "in every kind of literature and wisdom" (v. 17). That's a gift, not a compromise. But the gift was to a man who'd already proven he wouldn't bend on what mattered. You can't skip verse 8 and expect verse 17 to show up.

### Biblical Support

**1 Corinthians 9:19-23** (CSB)
> Although I am free from all and not anyone's slave, I have made myself a slave to everyone, in order to win more people. To the Jews I became like a Jew, to win Jews; to those under the law, like one under the law—though I myself am not under the law—to win those under the law. To those who are without the law, like one without the law—though I am not without God's law but under the law of Christ—to win those without the law. To the weak I became weak, in order to win the weak. I have become all things to all people, so that I may by every possible means save some. Now I do all this because of the gospel, so that I may share in the blessings.

**Word Study:** *kerdainō* (κερδαίνω) — "to gain, to win." Paul's flexibility wasn't compromise; it was tactical. He learned the language of every audience without surrendering the gospel. Daniel did the same in Babylon.

**Philippians 4:8** (CSB)
> Finally brothers and sisters, whatever is true, whatever is honorable, whatever is just, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely, whatever is commendable—if there is any moral excellence and if there is anything praiseworthy—dwell on these things.

**Word Study:** *logizomai* (λογίζομαι) — "to reckon, to consider, to think about." Paul's not saying only read Christian books. He's saying test everything against this grid. Daniel studied Chaldean literature and filtered it through the law of Moses. That's the model.

**Acts 17:22-23** (CSB)
> Paul stood in the middle of the Areopagus and said, "People of Athens! I see that you are extremely religious in every respect. For as I was passing through and observing the objects of your worship, I even found an altar on which was inscribed, 'To an Unknown God.' Therefore, what you worship in ignorance, this I proclaim to you."

**Word Study:** *katanoeō* (κατανοέω) — "to observe carefully, to consider closely." Paul studied Athens. He quoted their poets (v. 28). He didn't retreat. He engaged, and then he preached Christ crucified.

### Personal Application

This week, ask Lucy or Lily what they're learning in school that doesn't sound right to them. Don't freak out if they mention something woke or secular. Ask them why it sounds off, and walk them through how to think about it biblically. Teach them to engage the empire's language without bowing to the empire's gods. That skill will serve them for life.

## Q5. What can we learn from this?

### Answer

Daniel and his friends were ten times better than the best Babylon had (v. 20). That's not hyperbole. The text is clear: when Nebuchadnezzar interviewed them, they were in a different league.

Here's what we learn: faithfulness to God doesn't make you less competent in the world's systems. It makes you *more* competent. Because the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom (Proverbs 9:10), and wisdom makes you better at everything — management, strategy, engineering, parenting, finance, all of it.

The world wants you to believe the opposite. It wants you to think that biblical ethics are a handicap, that you'll fall behind if you don't cut corners, that compromise is the price of excellence. Daniel proves that's a lie. The guy who wouldn't eat the king's food ended up advising the king better than the guys who did.

This isn't prosperity gospel. It's not "be faithful and you'll get rich." It's "be faithful and you'll be useful, and God will put you where He wants you." Daniel didn't set out to become prime minister. He set out to stay clean. God did the rest.

### Biblical Support

**Proverbs 22:29** (CSB)
> Do you see a person skilled in his work? He will stand in the presence of kings. He will not stand in the presence of the unknown.

**Word Study:** *māhîr* (מָהִיר) — "skillful, quick, ready, expert." Excellence in your craft isn't worldliness. It's worship. And it opens doors.

**Colossians 3:23-24** (CSB)
> Whatever you do, do it from the heart, as something done for the Lord and not for people, knowing that you will receive the reward of an inheritance from the Lord. You serve the Lord Christ.

**Word Study:** *psychē* (ψυχή) — "soul, heart, life." Work from the soul means work that flows from who you are in Christ, not from fear of man or hunger for applause. That's the kind of work that compounds into excellence.

**Daniel 6:3** (CSB)
> Daniel distinguished himself above the administrators and satraps because he had an extraordinary spirit, so the king planned to set him over the whole realm.

**Word Study:** *yattîr* (יַתִּיר) — "extraordinary, surpassing, excellent." This wasn't Daniel in chapter 6 being lucky. This was the fruit of a life that started in chapter 1 refusing to compromise. Faithfulness over decades produces "extraordinary."

### Personal Application

This week, pick one thing at work you've been half-assing because "it doesn't really matter" or "nobody notices." A code comment you skipped. A test you didn't write. A meeting you checked out of. Do that thing with full attention, as if you're delivering it to Christ Himself, because you are (Colossians 3:23). See if it changes how you feel about Monday mornings.

## Q6. Our lesson here?

### Answer

Same answer, different angle: the lesson is that you don't have to choose between faithfulness and effectiveness. Daniel didn't. He was both. And the text goes out of its way to show that the two went together, not in spite of each other.

The temptation is to think that if you're faithful, you'll be marginalized. If you don't play the game, you won't get the promotion. If you don't bend, you'll break. Daniel's story says no. Bend on what doesn't matter (learn the language, take the new name, study the literature). Don't bend on what does (worship, diet tied to covenant law, ultimate allegiance). God will honor the difference.

We see this in verse 20: Nebuchadnezzar consulted them on "every matter of wisdom and understanding." These weren't ceremonial advisors. They were the go-to team. Why? Because they were useful. And they were useful because they were faithful.

The world doesn't need more Christians who are nice but incompetent, or competent but compromised. It needs Daniel: both, at the same time, for decades.

### Biblical Support

**Matthew 5:13-16** (CSB)
> You are the salt of the earth. But if the salt should lose its taste, how can it be made salty? It's no longer good for anything but to be thrown out and trampled under people's feet. No one lights a lamp and puts it under a basket, but rather on a lampstand, and it gives light for all who are in the house. In the same way, let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father in heaven.

**Word Study:** *mōrainō* (μωραίνω) — "to become foolish, to lose flavor." Salt that doesn't preserve or flavor is useless. A Christian who's faithfully incompetent is lampstand under a basket. Daniel was salty *and* bright.

**Titus 2:9-10** (CSB)
> Slaves are to submit to their masters in everything, and to be well-pleasing, not talking back or stealing, but demonstrating utter faithfulness, so that they may adorn the teaching of God our Savior in everything.

**Word Study:** *kosmeō* (κοσμέω) — "to adorn, to make beautiful, to decorate." Paul's point: your excellence and integrity make the gospel look good. Daniel's competence made Yahweh look good to Nebuchadnezzar. That's the mission.

**1 Thessalonians 4:11-12** (CSB)
> to seek to lead a quiet life, to mind your own business, and to work with your own hands, as we commanded you, so that you may behave properly in the presence of outsiders and not be dependent on anyone.

**Word Study:** *euschēmonōs* (εὐσχημόνως) — "decently, properly, becomingly." The goal is that unbelievers see Christians and think, "I don't agree with them, but they're not lazy or weird or incompetent." That was Daniel.

### Personal Application

This week, check your GitHub PRs or work output from the last month. Are you delivering the kind of work that makes your boss wonder what you're on, or are you coasting? If you're coasting, identify one deliverable this week that you'll overcomplete — not for recognition, but because you're representing a King and you're not going to let Him look bad.

## Q7. What are the lessons that can be gleaned from this chapter?

### Answer

Here's the summary, the whole arc of Daniel 1 in a few lines:

**Decide before the test.** Daniel "determined" before the food arrived. Predecision is the only kind that survives pressure. If you're making it up on the fly, you've already lost.

**Engage respectfully, resist firmly.** Daniel didn't retreat from Babylon, and he didn't let Babylon own him. He learned the language, studied the texts, mastered the system — and kept his soul. That's the model.

**Trust God with outcomes.** Daniel proposed the test and handed the results to God. He didn't manipulate, didn't hedge, didn't have a backup plan. God made him healthier. He didn't have to. That's faith.

**Excellence is worship.** Daniel and his friends were ten times better than their peers because they feared God more than Nebuchadnezzar. Faithfulness didn't limit their effectiveness — it multiplied it.

**God uses faithful exiles.** Daniel didn't choose to go to Babylon. God sent him. And for the next seventy years, God used a teenager who wouldn't eat the wrong food to counsel kings, interpret dreams, survive lions, and point an empire toward the God of Israel.

The lesson is this: we're all in exile. The question is whether we'll be Daniel or just another Babylonian bureaucrat with a Hebrew name.

### Biblical Support

**Romans 12:1-2** (CSB)
> Therefore, brothers and sisters, in view of the mercies of God, I urge you to present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and pleasing to God; this is your true worship. Do not be conformed to this age, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind, so that you may discern what is the good, pleasing, and perfect will of God.

**Word Study:** *syschēmatizō* (συσχηματίζω) — "to conform, to fashion oneself according to." The prefix *syn-* means "together with" — this is peer pressure at the identity level. Paul says don't. Daniel shows us how.

**1 Peter 2:11-12** (CSB)
> Dear friends, I urge you as strangers and exiles to abstain from sinful desires that wage war against the soul. Conduct yourselves honorably among the Gentiles, so that when they slander you as evildoers, they will observe your good works and will glorify God on the day he visits.

**Word Study:** *paroikos* (πάροικος) — "sojourner, exile, stranger." Peter's writing to Christians in Asia Minor. But the language is Daniel's. You're not home. Live like it.

**Jeremiah 29:4-7** (CSB)
> "This is what the LORD of Armies, the God of Israel, says to all the exiles I deported from Jerusalem to Babylon: 'Build houses and settle down. Plant gardens and eat their produce. Find wives for yourselves, and have sons and daughters. Find wives for your sons and give your daughters to men in marriage so that they may bear sons and daughters. Multiply there; do not decrease. Pursue the well-being of the city I have deported you to. Pray to the LORD on its behalf, for when it thrives, you will thrive.'"

**Word Study:** *shālôm* (שָׁלוֹם) — "peace, well-being, prosperity." This is the context Daniel lived in. God told the exiles: don't retreat, don't rebel — thrive, build, work for the city's good. Daniel took that seriously. So should we.

### Personal Application

This week, sit down with Wendy and write out the three lines you won't cross in the next ten years — at work, with money, with the kids' education, whatever. Be specific. Don't write "honor God." Write "I won't lie to a client to close a deal," or "I won't let a coach schedule Sunday games without pushing back," or "I won't let the kids skip church for travel ball." Make the call now, together, and put it somewhere you'll both see it when the test shows up.

## Context & Theological Grounding

### Historical Context

Daniel 1 opens in 605 BC, the third year of Jehoiakim's reign. Nebuchadnezzar's first siege of Jerusalem resulted in the deportation of Judah's brightest young men — the nobility, the royal family, the future leaders. This was strategic: break a nation by capturing its next generation and reprogramming them.

The "chief eunuch" (Ashpenaz) likely means Daniel and his friends were made eunuchs, which would've disqualified them from full participation in Israel's worship (Deuteronomy 23:1). If so, Daniel's exile wasn't just geographic — it was covenantal. And yet he stayed faithful.

The three-year training program (v. 5) was immersive: new language, new curriculum, new religion, new names. "Belteshazzar" means "Bel, protect his life" — literally invoking a pagan god. Daniel couldn't control the name. He could control the diet. That's where he drew the line.

### Theological Themes

**Sovereignty and Exile.** Verse 2 says "the Lord handed Jehoiakim over." This wasn't geopolitical bad luck. God exiled Judah because they broke the covenant (2 Kings 24:3-4). But even in judgment, God preserved a remnant and put Daniel exactly where he needed to be.

**Covenant Faithfulness in a Pagan System.** The dietary laws weren't arbitrary. They marked Israel as holy, set apart, distinct from the nations. Eating the king's food would've meant food sacrificed to idols (likely) and animals prohibited by Levitical law (definitely). Daniel refused to blur the line.

**Common Grace and Excellence.** Verse 17 says "God gave them knowledge and understanding." This is common grace — God gifting His people to serve even a pagan empire. Daniel didn't just survive Babylon. He made Babylon better. That's the calling for Christians in exile.

**Eschatological Hope.** Verse 21 is easy to miss: "Daniel remained until the first year of King Cyrus." That's 539 BC, the year Persia conquered Babylon and Cyrus issued the decree to rebuild the temple. Daniel outlasted the empire. Faithfulness has a long horizon.

### Canonical Context

Daniel's story echoes Joseph in Genesis 39–41: both are exiles in a foreign land, both are given favor and wisdom by God, both rise to advise pagan kings, both remain faithful to Yahweh when compromise would've been easier.

The New Testament picks up the "exile" metaphor in 1 Peter 1:1, calling believers "exiles scattered throughout" the world. Daniel is the prototype. Jesus is the ultimate faithful exile — the one who didn't compromise, who spoke truth to power, who trusted God with the outcome, and who was vindicated not in ten days but in three.
