# Word Studies — Selection and Format

Greek and Hebrew word studies are a non-negotiable part of every answer block. Done well they illuminate; done lazily they're decoration.

## How to choose words

Pick 1–3 words per question. Good candidates:

1. **Theologically loaded terms.** *agapē*, *pistis*, *dikaiosynē*, *chesed*, *shalom*, *ruach*, *mysterion*.
2. **Words English translation flattens.** Where one Greek word covers what English needs three for, or vice versa. Example: *plērōma* ("fullness") vs the English "filled up" which loses the cosmic scope.
3. **Words the false teachers / opposition co-opted.** Paul redefines *gnōsis* and *sophia* in Col 2 to disarm the proto-Gnostics. Naming that is a payload.
4. **Verbal-aspect markers.** When a perfect-tense participle (`peplēromenoi` — "having been filled and remaining filled") changes the theology, study the verb form.
5. **Words with the same root in OT and NT.** Greek *peripateō* ↔ Hebrew *halak*. Drawing the line across testaments grounds canonical context.

Avoid:

- ❌ Words chosen just because they're famous. Don't include *agapē* in every study reflexively.
- ❌ Hapax legomena unless the rarity itself is the point.
- ❌ Words whose study reduces to "this means love." If the payload is one English word, drop the entry.

## Format

```markdown
- **<Lang>: *transliteration* (Original Script)** — "gloss." Payload sentence(s).
```

### Required components

| Component | Required | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Language tag | yes | `Greek:` or `Hebrew:` |
| Transliteration in italics | yes | `*peripateō*` |
| Original script in parens | yes | `(περιπατέω)` |
| Brief gloss in quotes | yes | `"to walk, to conduct one's life."` |
| Theological payload | yes | The *why this matters* sentence(s). |

### Optional but encouraged

- Cross-language pair: `Hebrew equivalent *halak* (הָלַךְ).`
- Verse echoes: `Paul keeps coming back to it (Col 1:12; 2:7; 3:15, 17; 4:2).`
- Verb form note: `perfect passive participle, "having been filled and continuing in the state of being filled."`

## Examples

✅ Good:

> **Greek: *cheirographon* (χειρόγραφον)** — "handwritten certificate of debt, IOU." In the Greco-Roman world, a *cheirographon* was a personally signed promissory note. The image in v. 14 is staggering: my own signature was on the debt against me — I created it by my sin — and Christ took the document and *nailed* it to the cross. Debt: cancelled, publicly, finally.

✅ Good (paired):

> **Greek: *skia / sōma* (σκιά / σῶμα)** — "shadow / body (substance)." A shadow is *real* — it points to something — but it has no substance of its own. The OT ceremonial law was a real shadow of a coming reality. Once the body (Christ) arrived, clinging to the shadow is preferring the photograph to the person.

❌ Bad:

> **Greek: *agape*** — love.

❌ Bad:

> **Hebrew: *shema*** — "to hear." This is an important word.

## Sourcing

- **Lexical baseline**: BDAG (Greek), HALOT (Hebrew). Don't cite them inline — internalize.
- **Free online fallbacks**: STEP Bible (`stepbible.org`), Bible Hub (`biblehub.com`), Blue Letter Bible (`blueletterbible.org`). Use `web_extract` for live lookups.
- **Sanity check**: if the word's "meaning" you wrote is repeated verbatim from the first hit on Bible Hub, dig deeper. The payload should reflect the *passage* you're working in, not a generic gloss.

## Pre-commit checklist for word studies

- [ ] 1–3 entries per question (not 0, not 5).
- [ ] Original script + transliteration both present.
- [ ] Payload sentence(s) explain *why this matters in this passage*.
- [ ] No reflexive defaults (*agape*, *pistis*) unless they're truly load-bearing.
